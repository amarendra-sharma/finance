Chapter 1 · Foundations

What Finance Is

📖 14 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 4 exercises + quiz

Finance is what you do when money and time collide. Every financial decision — by a person, a company, or a government — is a choice about giving up something now to get something later, or the reverse, in a world where the future is uncertain. That is the whole subject in one sentence. Everything else is detail, and this book is the detail.

Imagine you are handed $10,000 today, with one rule: you cannot spend it this year. You can put it under a mattress, lend it to a friend who promises $10,600 next year, buy a share of a company, or hand it to a bank. A year from now you will be judged only on what you have. What should you do?

You cannot answer without weighing three things you feel instinctively but have probably never named: that a dollar next year is worth less to you than a dollar today, that a promise from a shaky friend is worth less than the same promise from a solid one, and that some bets pay more precisely because they might pay nothing. Those three instincts — time, risk, and the price of bearing them — are the foundations of finance. By the last chapter you will reason about them with formulas; at present you reason about them intuitively. This book connects the two.

The three questions

Strip away the jargon and all of finance is three recurring questions. A household faces them; so does a large corporation; so does a national treasury. They are simply asked at different scales.

The investment question
What should we own?

Of all the things you could put money into — a degree, a factory, a stock, a bond, a rental property — which are worth more than they cost? In corporate finance this is the capital budgeting decision; for you it is every choice to buy an asset instead of spending.

The financing question
How should we pay for it?

With your own money (equity) or with borrowed money (debt)? A firm chooses between issuing stock and issuing bonds; you choose between a down payment and a loan. This is the capital structure decision, and it changes who bears the risk and who keeps the reward.

The cash‑management question
How do we not run out of money in the meantime?

A profitable business can still fail if it cannot pay its bills when they fall due. Managing the timing of cash in and cash out — working capital — is the discipline without which the other two decisions come to nothing.

Retain this framing. When you meet net present value in Chapter 7, it will be a tool for the investment question. When you meet the cost of capital in Chapter 9, it answers the financing question. The book returns to these three questions, asked with increasing precision.

The first force: money has a time value

Ask anyone whether they would rather have $1,000 today or $1,000 in a year, and they choose today — and they are right, for reasons deeper than impatience. A dollar today can be put to work: deposited, lent, or invested so that it becomes more than a dollar by next year. That earning power is called the time value of money, and the rate at which money grows over time is the interest rate, usually written $r$.

If a dollar grows at rate $r$ per year, then one dollar today becomes $1+r$ dollars next year. Two years out it becomes $(1+r)^2$, because the interest itself earns interest — the process we will call compounding. Turned around: a dollar you will receive next year is worth only $1/(1+r)$ dollars to you today, because that smaller amount, invested now, would grow into the dollar. This one idea — that value depends on when a cash flow arrives — is the single most important idea in the book. We give it a full chapter next.

The two moves you will make a thousand times
Future value  →  FV = PV × (1 + r)n   // push a dollar forward in time
Present value  →  PV = FV / (1 + r)n   // pull a future dollar back to today

The financial system

Money has a time value because it can be put to work — but where? The answer is an extensive system for moving money from those who have more than they need at present (savers) to people who can do something productive with it (borrowers and firms). That system has three principal components.

Financial markets

Places — mostly electronic now — where financial assets are bought and sold. The stock market trades ownership shares of companies; the bond market, far larger, trades loans. Markets set prices, and a price is the market’s collective answer to “what is this future stream of cash worth today?”

Financial intermediaries

Institutions that stand between saver and borrower: banks, insurers, pension funds, mutual funds. You deposit $5,000; the bank lends it to a business; you never meet the business. Intermediaries exist because they are better than you at two hard jobs — judging who will repay, and pooling many small savers so that one borrower’s default does not sink any single lender.

The instruments themselves

The contracts that get traded: a stock is a share of ownership and its uncertain profits; a bond is a promise to repay a loan on a schedule; a derivative is a contract whose value depends on another asset. Chapters 5, 6, and 12 take these apart one by one. For now, notice what they share: every one of them is a claim on future cash, which is why valuing future cash is the skill the whole book is built to teach.

The one job of the whole system

Channel savings to their most productive use, and price the risk of doing so. When the system works, a good idea with no money finds money with no ideas. When it breaks — 2008 — the channel clogs, and the damage spreads far beyond finance because everything real is financed.

Real assets and financial assets

It helps to keep two categories straight. Real assets are the productive things themselves — factories, farmland, patents, an education, a delivery truck. They generate the economy’s actual output. Financial assets — stocks, bonds, loans — are claims on the income that real assets produce. A share of a bakery is not the ovens; it is the right to a share of what the ovens earn.

This distinction resolves a good deal of confusion. When the stock market falls, no ovens vanish; the market has only revised its estimate of what the ovens will earn. Financial assets are society’s ledger for dividing up the output of its real assets — and finance, as a field, is largely the art of pricing that ledger correctly.

The second force: risk

If time were the only force, finance would be arithmetic. The complication — and the reason it is a real subject — is that the future is uncertain. The friend might not repay. The company might not profit. The word for this is risk, and finance takes a specific, initially surprising stance on it: risk is not merely to be avoided, it is to be priced.

Because most people dislike uncertainty, they must be paid to bear it. That payment is the risk premium — the extra expected return a risky investment must offer over a safe one to attract any takers. It is why stocks have historically returned more than government bonds, and why a loan to a shaky borrower carries a higher interest rate than a loan to a solid one. Risk and return are inseparable, and Chapters 8 through 12 are in large part a sustained study of their relationship.

The two forces, together

Every price in finance is an answer to one question: what is this stream of future cash worth today, given how long we must wait for it and how uncertain it is? Waiting is handled by discounting for time; uncertainty is handled by demanding a risk premium. Learn to answer that question and you can value anything — a bond, a business, a lottery, a lease, your own degree.

Why this matters to you

You will make a handful of financial decisions in your life that dwarf all the others: how much to borrow for school, whether and when to buy a home, how to invest for a retirement forty years away, how much debt to carry and at what rate. Each is an instance of the three questions, subject to the two forces. People who understand the underlying principles make these decisions with confidence; those who do not bear a lasting cost in unfavourable rates, forgone compounding, and avoidable risk.

This is not a course in becoming wealthy quickly, and it will not recommend particular securities. It is a course in thinking clearly about money and time under uncertainty — the one skill underneath all the specific decisions. The personal‑finance chapters at the end (13 and 14) turn the theory directly on your own life; everything before them builds the tools.

A look ahead

  • Chapters 2–3 make the time value of money exact: present value, future value, and the compounding of returns.
  • Chapters 4–6 use it to value real things — loans and mortgages, bonds, and whole companies.
  • Chapters 7–9 are corporate finance: choosing investments (NPV), measuring risk and return, and paying for it all (the cost of capital).
  • Chapters 10–12 turn to markets and investing: diversification, whether markets can be beaten, and options.
  • Chapters 13–14 bring it home to budgeting, debt, taxes, and building your own wealth.

You are ready for Chapter 2, where the one idea in this chapter — that value depends on timing — becomes a precise and surprisingly powerful tool.

Where this came from

The time value of money is old. Medieval merchants and the mathematician Fibonacci (1202) computed present values to compare trades; Renaissance bankers priced loans with compound interest tables. But finance became a rigorous, mathematical field only in the twentieth century: Irving Fisher formalized interest and capital in the 1930s, Harry Markowitz put risk on a mathematical footing in 1952, and by the 1970s the pricing of options had become precise enough to win Nobel Prizes. This book walks that same path — intuition first, then the mathematics that made it exact — in a single course.

Exercise 1.1 Easy

For each item, say whether it is a real asset or a financial asset: (a) a coffee‑shop’s espresso machine, (b) 50 shares of that coffee shop’s stock, (c) a $20,000 car loan you took out, from the bank’s point of view, (d) a college degree.

Solution

(a) Real — a productive physical thing. (b) Financial — a claim on the shop’s future profits. (c) Financial — to the bank the loan is an asset: a claim on your future repayments. (d) Real — human capital; it raises your future productive earning power, even though you cannot sell it.

Note the pattern: real assets produce income; financial assets are claims on it. The same contract (the loan) is a liability to you and an asset to the bank — every financial asset is somebody else’s obligation.

Exercise 1.2 Easy

The interest rate is 6%. (a) What is $500 today worth in one year? (b) What is $500 to be received in one year worth today? Explain in one sentence why the two answers differ.

Solution

(a) Future value: 500 × 1.06 = $530. (b) Present value: 500 / 1.06 = $471.70.

They differ because moving money forward in time adds interest (multiply by 1+r), while moving it backward removes the interest it would have earned (divide by 1+r). Today’s $471.70, invested at 6%, becomes exactly $500 next year — which is what “present value” means.

Exercise 1.3 Medium

A startup with a brilliant product has no cash; a retiree has $50,000 in savings and no ideas. Describe, using the three parts of the financial system, one path by which the retiree’s savings could end up funding the startup — and name the risk the retiree is being paid to bear.

Solution

One path: the retiree buys shares of a venture‑capital or mutual fund (an intermediary) through the market; the fund uses pooled money to buy the startup’s stock (the instrument), delivering the savings to the firm. Alternatively the retiree buys a bond the startup issues.

The risk being priced is that the startup fails and the investment is lost. The retiree accepts that risk only in exchange for a risk premium — an expected return above what a safe government bond would pay. The intermediary provides value by assessing which startups merit investment and by pooling many of them so that no single failure is ruinous.

Exercise 1.4 Hard

“When the stock market crashes, the country becomes poorer by exactly the amount of market value that disappeared.” Using the real‑asset / financial‑asset distinction, explain what is right and what is wrong about this claim.

Solution

Wrong, in an immediate physical sense: a crash destroys no real assets. The factories, workers, and patents are all still there the morning after. What changed is the market’s estimate of the future income those real assets will produce — a repricing of financial claims, not a disappearance of productive capacity.

Right, in a forward‑looking sense: the price drop usually reflects genuinely worse expectations about future output (a coming recession, say). And there are real feedback effects — a crash can make firms and households cut spending (a wealth effect) and make financing harder to get, which can actually reduce future real output.

So the crash is better read as a signal and an amplifier of expected real losses than as the loss itself. The distinction between the ledger (financial assets) and the thing being tallied (real assets) is exactly what keeps this straight.

The Time Value of Money

📖 28 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Chapter 1 established that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. This chapter turns that instinct into a precise tool — one sharp enough to compare any two streams of cash, arriving at any dates, on a single common ruler: their value today. One operation, called discounting, underlies the entire method, and once it is mastered it will value a bond, a business, a mortgage, or a lottery prize. Every chapter that follows is an application of it.

You win a lottery, and the clerk offers a choice: $1,000,000 in cash now, or $50,000 a year for thirty years — $1,500,000 in total. The second amount is half a million dollars larger, and most people take it. Most people are mistaken, and by the end of this chapter the reason, and the exact magnitude of the error, will be clear.

The $1.5 million is an illusion of addition: it sums dollars that arrive in different years as though they were the same quantity. They are not. A dollar arriving in year thirty is worth far less today than a dollar in hand, because the dollar in hand can be invested and allowed to grow for thirty years. To compare the two offers honestly, every future payment must be brought back to the present — and once that is done, the larger pile frequently falls below the cash.

Pushing money forward: future value

Begin with the straightforward direction. Place $PV$ dollars in an account earning $r$ per year. After one year the balance is $PV(1+r)$. Leave it a second year and the entire balance grows again, to $PV(1+r)^2$: the interest earned in the first year itself earns interest in the second. This process — earning interest on previously earned interest — is called compounding, and it is developed fully in Chapter 3. After $n$ years the balance is:

Future value of a single amount
FV = PV × (1 + r)n   // each year multiplies the whole balance by (1+r)

The exponent is the source of the effect. Growth is not linear — adding $r$ each year — but exponential, multiplying by $1+r$ each year, and over decades the distinction is large. A single $1,000 deposit at 7 percent becomes $1,967 after ten years and $7,612 after thirty; the second thirty-year figure is nearly four times the ten-year one, though the time is only three times as long. Chapter 3 examines this growth in detail. The present chapter needs the reverse direction.

Pulling money back: present value

Now reverse the question. Suppose a fixed amount $FV$ is promised at a future date. What is that promise worth today? It must be the amount that, invested now at rate $r$, would grow into exactly $FV$ by the promised date. Solving the future-value relation for $PV$ gives:

Present value of a single future amount
PV = FV / (1 + r)n   // divide out the growth the money would have earned

This operation — converting a future dollar into its worth today — is called discounting, and $r$ in this role is the discount rate. The factor $1/(1+r)^n$ is the discount factor; for any positive rate it is less than one, which is the mathematics stating that future money is worth less. The farther away the cash flow (larger $n$) or the higher the rate, the smaller the discount factor, and the less a future dollar is worth now. The graph makes the decay concrete: it plots the present value of a single future dollar against the number of years until it arrives, at two discount rates.

Two features deserve attention. First, every curve begins at one, because a dollar available today is worth exactly a dollar. Second, the decline is steeper and deeper at the higher rate: at 10 percent, a dollar arriving in thirty years is worth about six cents today, whereas at 4 percent it is worth about thirty cents. The discount rate governs how severely the future is penalised.

Definition
Present value (PV)

The value today of a cash flow, or of a whole stream of cash flows, to be received in the future, obtained by discounting each future amount at the appropriate rate. Present value is the universal common denominator of finance: it allows cash flows that arrive at different times to be added and compared.

Worked example
A promise three years out

A creditworthy borrower will pay $5,000 in exactly three years. Comparable safe investments earn 6 percent. What is the promise worth today?

PV = 5000 / (1.06)3 = 5000 / 1.1910 = $4,198.10

One should be willing to pay up to $4,198.10 for this promise and no more, because $4,198.10 invested at 6 percent grows into exactly $5,000 in three years. A seller offering the promise for $3,900 is offering it below its value, and it should be bought.

Valuing a whole stream: present value is additive

Real investments rarely pay only once. A bond pays interest each year and principal at maturity; a company generates cash for decades. The property that makes such streams tractable is that present values add: the value today of a stream of cash flows is the sum of the present values of its individual payments. If a project pays $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n$ at the ends of years $1$ through $n$, then:

PV = C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + … + Cn/(1+r)n

Each term brings one future payment back to today; their sum is the value of the whole stream now. This single equation, in one form or another, lies behind every valuation in this book.

Worked example
Three years of a company’s cash

Northwind Components, the firm used throughout this book, is expected to generate free cash flow of $120m, $130m, and $140m over the next three years. At a discount rate of 9 percent, what is that three-year stream worth today?

PV = 120/1.09 + 130/1.092 + 140/1.093

= 110.09 + 109.42 + 108.11 = $327.62m

Note that the $140m in year three, though the largest payment, contributes the least in present value, because it is discounted over the most years. Timing matters as much as magnitude — the principle that an entire valuation chapter (Chapter 6) later rests upon.

Where does the discount rate come from?

The formula requires a value for $r$, and choosing it well is most of the craft of valuation. The discount rate is fundamentally an opportunity cost: the return available on the next-best alternative investment of comparable risk. If safe government bonds yield 4 percent, a safe future cash flow should be discounted at 4 percent, because 4 percent is what is given up by committing money to this promise instead.

Risk raises the rate. A cash flow that is uncertain should be discounted at a higher rate than a certain one, because the next-best alternative of comparable risk earns more — the risk premium introduced in Chapter 1. A risky corporate cash flow might be discounted at 10 percent while a Treasury payment is discounted at 4 percent. The discount rate therefore encodes both forces of finance at once: the pure time value, captured by the safe rate, and a premium for bearing risk. Chapters 8 and 9 construct the machinery that determines the appropriate premium; for the present, the intuition suffices: riskier future cash is discounted more heavily.

Nominal and real rates: the role of inflation

A quoted interest rate usually measures growth in dollars, not in purchasing power. The nominal rate is the stated rate — the percentage by which a dollar balance grows. Inflation is the rate at which the general price level rises, eroding what each dollar buys. The real rate is the growth in purchasing power that remains once inflation is removed. If a savings account pays a nominal 8 percent while prices rise 3 percent, the balance buys only about 5 percent more goods at year end; the real return is roughly 5 percent.

Real and nominal rates (the Fisher relation)
(1 + rreal) = (1 + rnominal) / (1 + inflation)   // approximately, r_real ≈ r_nominal − inflation

The exact calculation for the example is $1.08/1.03 - 1 = 4.85\%$, close to the 5 percent the approximation gives. The practical rule that governs every valuation is one of consistency: discount nominal cash flows (actual future dollars) at a nominal rate, and real cash flows (expressed in today’s purchasing power) at a real rate. Mixing the two — discounting inflated future dollars at a real rate, for instance — is a common and material error.

The master recipe

To value anything: (1) forecast its future cash flows, (2) choose a discount rate that reflects their risk, (3) discount each flow back to today, and (4) add the results. Every valuation in this book — bonds, stocks, whole companies, a university degree — is a special case of these four steps. The chapters differ only in how the cash flows are forecast and how the rate is chosen.

From present value to a decision: net present value

Present value states what a stream of future cash is worth. To decide whether to acquire it, that worth is compared with the price. The difference is net present value:

NPV = (PV of everything it pays) − (what it costs today)

A positive NPV means the asset is worth more than it costs, and it should be accepted; a negative NPV means the reverse. This is the entire logic of the investment decision, and the live sheet computes it directly. Chapter 7 makes net present value the centrepiece of corporate finance; it appears here, several chapters early, because it follows immediately from discounting.

And the lottery. Discounting the thirty annual payments of $50,000 at a modest 6 percent gives a present value of about $688,000 — well below the $1,000,000 cash. The larger $1.5 million total is worth less today than the smaller immediate offer. The cash should be taken. Exercise 2.4 develops this result in full.

(c) The discount factor compounds the rate over ten years, so a higher rate is magnified by the exponent: 1.1010 is far larger than 1.0410. The ten-year point on the graph sits much lower for the 10% curve than for the 4% curve, which is why the same future payment is worth almost $2,900 less at the higher rate.

Exercise 2.6 Hard

An investment is expected to return a nominal 7% next year, while inflation is expected to be 4%. (a) Estimate the real return with the approximation and compute it exactly. (b) Why does a saver ultimately care about the real figure rather than the nominal one?

Solution

(a) Approximation: 7% − 4% = 3%. Exact: 1.07/1.04 − 1 = 2.88%.

(b) A saver invests in order to consume later, and consumption depends on purchasing power, not on the dollar count. A nominal 7% that is entirely consumed by inflation leaves the saver no better able to buy goods; only the real return — here about 2.9% — measures the genuine gain. This is also why nominal cash flows must be discounted at a nominal rate and real cash flows at a real rate.

Where this came from

Discounting is old arithmetic put to new use. Leonardo of Pisa (“Fibonacci”), in Liber Abaci (1202), worked present-value problems to compare business deals across time. The idea became the organising principle of finance only in 1930, when the Yale economist Irving Fisher, in The Theory of Interest, argued that the value of any asset is the present value of the income it will produce, and that investment decisions should be made by comparing present values. He also formalised the relation between nominal and real rates that still bears his name. Every valuation model in this book descends from these claims.

Compounding & Growth

📖 28 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Compounding is the mechanism underlying every figure in this book. It is also the most frequently underestimated force in personal finance, because intuition tends to reason in linear terms while compounding is exponential. This chapter sets out the mechanism precisely — how compounding frequency, stated rates, and elapsed time interact — and establishes its principal practical consequence: in the accumulation of wealth, the timing of saving matters more than its amount.

Two individuals, Ana and Ben, each save $200 a month at a 7 percent return. Ana begins at age 25 and stops contributing at 35: ten years, and $24,000 of her own money in total. Ben begins at 35 and contributes every month until 65: thirty years, and $72,000 of his own money. Ben contributes three times as much, over three times as long. At age 65, which of them has accumulated more?

Ana does, by a wide margin. Her $24,000, granted a forty-year period in which to compound, exceeds Ben’s $72,000. The result appears counterintuitive until compounding is understood, after which it is evident. This is the reason the chapter appears so early in the book, and the reason the companion arena allows the comparison to be run directly.

Simple and compound interest

Under simple interest, only the original principal earns a return, and growth follows a straight line: $1,000 at 10 percent simple interest gains $100 each year indefinitely. Under compound interest, each period’s interest is added to the balance and itself earns interest in subsequent periods. Growth is then exponential: the curve rises gradually at first and steeply thereafter. The distinction is the single most consequential idea in long-horizon finance.

Over a single year the difference is negligible. Over forty years it is substantial: $1,000 at 10 percent simple interest grows to $5,000, whereas the same sum compounded at 10 percent grows to approximately $45,000 — roughly nine times as much, from an identical rate. The entire difference is interest earning interest. The graph plots the two paths for a $1,000 deposit at 10 percent, and the widening gap between them is the whole of the matter.

The two paths begin together and separate slowly, then dramatically. For the first several years the lines are nearly indistinguishable, which is precisely why compounding is underestimated: its effect is modest over the horizons the mind inspects and overwhelming over the horizons that matter for retirement.

Compounding frequency: APR and EAR

Interest rates are quoted in a manner that requires care. A credit card advertises “18% APR”; a savings account, “2% compounded monthly.” The APR (annual percentage rate) is the periodic rate multiplied by the number of periods in a year — a stated, nominal figure that disregards compounding within the year. The amount actually earned or paid is the EAR (effective annual rate), which incorporates that within-year compounding.

From a stated APR to the effective rate
EAR = (1 + APR/m)m − 1   // m = compounding periods per year

An 18 percent APR compounding monthly has an EAR of $(1 + 0.18/12)^{12} - 1 = 19.56\%$: the effective cost is close to 20 percent, not 18. The more frequently interest compounds, the further the EAR exceeds the APR. Taking the frequency to its limit — compounding at every instant — yields continuous compounding, under which the future-value relation becomes $FV = PV \cdot e^{rn}$, where $e \approx 2.71828$ is the base of the natural logarithm. The Rule of 72

A calculator is not always available, and a reliable approximation exists for the question of how long a sum requires to double. Divide 72 by the interest rate expressed in percent:

Years to double ≈ 72 / (interest rate in %)

At 8 percent a sum doubles in about $72/8 = 9$ years; at 6 percent, about 12 years; at 3 percent, about 24. The rule is an approximation derived from the logarithm of two, accurate to within a few months across the range of rates encountered in practice. It renders the effect of higher returns concrete: the difference between a 3 percent and a 9 percent return is not a factor of three in outcome but the difference between doubling every eight years and doubling every twenty-four — over a career, the difference between five doublings and one.

Why timing dominates amount

Because a balance doubles on a fixed schedule set by the rate, the dollars invested earliest receive the greatest number of doublings. A dollar invested at 25 and held to 65 at 7 percent doubles roughly every ten years — four doublings, growing to about $16. The same dollar invested at 45 receives only two doublings, reaching about $4. Duration of investment, rather than its timing within a market cycle or even its amount, is what compounding rewards. This is the central practical result of the chapter.

Worked example
Compounding at the level of a company

Compounding governs corporate growth as well as personal saving. Suppose Northwind Components, with earnings of $1.75 per share, grows those earnings at 8 percent per year. Projecting forward:

Year 10: 1.75 × 1.0810 = $3.78  ·  Year 20: 1.75 × 1.0820 = $8.16  ·  Year 30: 1.75 × 1.0830 = $17.62

Earnings per share grow roughly tenfold over thirty years at a rate that appears unremarkable year to year. The same exponential mechanism that accumulates a retirement balance also drives the long-run value of a growing enterprise — the observation on which the dividend-growth valuation of Chapter 6 depends.

Real and nominal growth

The returns above are nominal — measured in dollars. As established in Chapter 2, dollars lose purchasing power to inflation, and the quantity of ultimate interest is the real return: growth in what a balance can purchase. The approximation introduced earlier applies here as well:

real return ≈ nominal return − inflation rate

A 7 percent nominal return during a period of 3 percent inflation produces growth in purchasing power of approximately 4 percent. The distinction is material over long horizons: a retirement projection that discounts or compounds in nominal terms while expressing goals in current purchasing power will overstate the balance’s future adequacy. Compounding also operates on inflation, steadily eroding idle cash — a consideration that favours investment over the holding of currency.

Where this came from

The mathematics of compound interest precedes modern finance by centuries. Jacob Bernoulli identified the constant e in 1683 through the study of continuous compounding, and the Rule of 72 appears in Luca Pacioli’s 1494 treatise, the work that also codified double-entry bookkeeping. What is modern is the emphasis: the recognition, formalised by twentieth-century economists and advanced by index-fund proponents such as John Bogle, that for ordinary savers the duration of investment and the level of costs dominate most other considerations.

Annuities, Loans & Mortgages

📖 28 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Most cash flows encountered in practice are not isolated amounts but streams of equal payments: a car loan, a mortgage, a monthly retirement contribution, a pension. Discounting each payment separately would be laborious, so finance provides a closed-form shortcut, the annuity formula, which reduces an entire stream to a single present value. This chapter develops that formula and applies it to the loan, savings, and mortgage calculations used throughout a financial life.

A buyer wants a $30,000 car, and the dealer offers terms of $560 a month for 60 months. The payments total $33,600. Whether this constitutes a favourable arrangement cannot be judged from the monthly figure alone; it requires reversing the arithmetic to find the interest rate that equates $30,000 today with sixty payments of $560. This chapter develops the method for computing that rate, for determining how much of each payment is interest, and for evaluating what the agreement entails.

The annuity: a stream of equal payments

An annuity is a series of equal cash flows $C$ paid at regular intervals for $n$ periods. Because each payment is identical, the sum of their present values has a compact closed form:

Present value of an annuity
PV = C × [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ] / r   // C = payment, r = rate per period, n = number of payments

The bracketed term is the annuity factor: the present value of $1 paid every period for $n$ periods. Multiplying any payment by this factor yields the value of the whole stream today. This is the figure 13.76 that appeared in the lottery calculation of Chapter 2 — the annuity factor for thirty years at 6 percent. The formula is not an arbitrary rule; it is the sum of the individual discount factors $1/(1+r) + 1/(1+r)^2 + \cdots + 1/(1+r)^n$, a geometric series that collapses to the expression above.

The timing convention matters. An ordinary annuity, assumed by the formula above, pays at the end of each period; most loans and bonds follow this convention. An annuity due pays at the beginning of each period, as rent and many leases do. Because each payment then arrives one period earlier, its present value is larger by a single factor of growth: $PV_{\text{due}} = PV_{\text{ordinary}} \times (1+r)$.

The future value of an annuity: regular saving

The same stream viewed forward rather than backward answers a different question: what does a series of equal deposits accumulate to by the end? This is the future value of an annuity, the basis of every regular savings plan:

Future value of an annuity
FV = C × [ (1 + r)n − 1 ] / r   // the accumulated value of n equal deposits
Worked example
A monthly retirement contribution

Suppose $500 is deposited at the end of each month for thirty years at a 7 percent annual rate, compounded monthly (a monthly rate of about 0.5833 percent over 360 months).

FV = 500 × [ (1.005833)360 − 1 ] / 0.005833 = 500 × 1220.1 = $610,050

The total contributed is $500 \times 360 = \$180{,}000$; the remaining $430,050 is accumulated investment growth. The disproportion between the two figures restates the central result of Chapter 3: over long horizons, compounding contributes more than the contributions themselves.

Perpetuities: payments without end

Allowing the number of payments to increase without limit reduces the annuity formula to a simple expression. A perpetuity — an equal payment that continues indefinitely — has present value:

PV = C / r

A payment of $100 a year in perpetuity, discounted at 5 percent, is worth $100/0.05 = \$2{,}000$ today. That an endless stream has a finite value follows directly from discounting: payments in the distant future are discounted so heavily that the infinite tail contributes almost nothing. A growing perpetuity, whose payment increases at a constant rate $g$ each period, generalises the result to $PV = C/(r-g)$, valid when $r > g$. This expression is the foundation of the dividend-growth valuation in Chapter 6, where a company is modelled as a growing perpetuity of cash returned to its owners.

Loans and amortization

A loan is an annuity viewed from the borrower’s perspective. The lender provides a present value — the loan amount $P$ — today, and the borrower repays it through a stream of equal payments. Setting the present value of those payments equal to the loan and solving for the payment gives:

The loan payment formula
Payment = P × r / [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ]   // r and n measured in the payment period (usually months)

Each payment performs two functions: it covers the interest accrued on the outstanding balance during that period, and it reduces the principal. This process of repaying a loan through scheduled payments that cover interest and retire principal is called amortization. Early in the loan the balance is large, so the interest due is large and only a small part of the fixed payment reduces principal. As the balance declines, the interest due falls and the principal portion grows, so the composition of the payment shifts steadily over the life of the loan. The graph shows this division for a $300,000 loan at 6.5 percent over thirty years, with a fixed monthly payment of about $1,896.

The two portions begin far apart — about $1,625 of the first payment is interest and only $271 reduces principal — and cross near the two-thirds point of the term, after which principal repayment dominates. This composition explains why the early years of a mortgage build little ownership in a property: most of each payment, at first, compensates the lender for the use of a large outstanding balance.

Where this came from

Annuity mathematics originated in the pricing of life contingencies. In 1693 the astronomer Edmond Halley constructed one of the first life tables and used it to value annuities on human lives for the English government, and in the eighteenth century Abraham de Moivre derived compact formulas for annuity values. The same mathematics now underlies consumer lending: the fixed-payment amortizing mortgage, standardised in the United States in the 1930s, applies the annuity formula that this chapter develops.

Bonds & Interest Rates

📖 30 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A bond is the most straightforward security to value, because its cash flows are specified in the contract: fixed coupon payments on a schedule, followed by repayment of the face value at maturity. Valuing one is an application of Chapter 2 — discount the cash flows and sum them. The defining feature of bonds is the inverse relationship at their centre: when interest rates rise, bond prices fall. Understanding precisely why opens the fixed-income market, the largest financial market in the world.

An investor buys a bond paying $50 a year and returning $1,000 at maturity. The following morning the central bank raises interest rates, and newly issued bonds pay $70. No buyer will now accept the $50 bond at the original price, since $70 is available elsewhere. The price of the $50 bond falls until its fixed payments represent the same return as the new $70 bonds. The investor took no action, held a default-free government bond, and nonetheless incurred a loss. That exposure is interest-rate risk, and this chapter makes it precise.

Anatomy of a bond

A bond is a loan made to a government or corporation, structured as a tradable security. Three terms define it. The face value, also called par and conventionally $1,000, is the amount repaid at the end. The coupon rate fixes the annual interest payment as a percentage of face value; the payment itself is the coupon. The maturity is the number of years until the face value is repaid. A “5 percent coupon, 10-year, $1,000 bond” pays a $50 coupon each year for ten years and then returns $1,000.

Definition
Yield to maturity (YTM)

The single discount rate that equates the present value of a bond’s cash flows to its market price — equivalently, the annual return earned by purchasing the bond now and holding it to maturity. The yield to maturity is the bond market’s measure of an interest rate, and it is the quantity referred to in a phrase such as “the 10-year yield.”

Two further rate measures should be distinguished from the yield to maturity. The coupon rate is fixed at issue and never changes. The current yield is the annual coupon divided by the current price — a rough income measure that ignores any gain or loss from the difference between price and face value. Only the yield to maturity accounts for both the coupon income and that gain or loss, which is why it is the standard measure of a bond’s return.

Pricing a bond is an application of present value

A bond’s price is the present value of its coupons, which form an annuity, plus the present value of its face value, a single amount, both discounted at the market yield $y$:

Bond price
Price = C × [1 − (1+y)−n] / y   +   F / (1+y)n   // coupons (annuity) + face value (lump sum)

The expression is the annuity formula of Chapter 4 combined with a single discounted amount. Two practical refinements apply. First, most bonds in the United States pay semiannually: a 5 percent coupon on a $1,000 bond pays $25 every six months, so the formula is applied with the coupon halved, the yield halved, and the number of periods doubled. Second, a zero-coupon bond pays no coupons at all; it is a single face-value payment at maturity, priced simply as $F/(1+y)^n$, and it always trades below face value. The inverse relationship between price and yield

The central fact of fixed income is that bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. The mechanism is visible in the pricing formula, where the yield appears in the denominator, so a larger yield produces a smaller price. The economic reasoning is more direct: a bond’s coupon is fixed. If market rates rise, that fixed coupon is unattractive beside newly issued bonds, so the existing bond’s price must fall until its yield matches the prevailing market rate. If rates fall, the existing bond’s above-market coupon becomes desirable, and its price rises. The graph plots this relationship for two bonds with the same 5 percent coupon but different maturities.

Two properties are evident. The relationship slopes downward, confirming the inverse link, and it is convex rather than straight: the price rises more for a given fall in yield than it falls for an equal rise. The 30-year curve is also far steeper than the 5-year curve, which is the graphical statement that long-maturity bonds are more sensitive to yield changes — the subject of duration below.

The pricing formula yields three cases, which the pricer labels. When the coupon rate equals the market yield, the bond trades at par, with price equal to face value. When the coupon exceeds the yield, buyers bid the price above face value, producing a premium bond. When the coupon is below the yield, the price falls below face value, producing a discount bond.

Interest-rate risk and duration

Bonds do not respond equally to a change in rates. A bond maturing in one year moves little when yields rise, because the principal is returned soon and reinvested at the new rate. A thirty-year bond, whose cash flows are fixed for decades, moves substantially. The general principle is that longer-maturity bonds are more sensitive to interest-rate changes. The precise measure of this sensitivity is duration: the approximate percentage by which a bond’s price changes for each one-percentage-point change in yield. Formally, duration is the present-value-weighted average time until a bond’s cash flows are received, which is why a longer maturity and a lower coupon both increase it.

A numerical illustration fixes the idea. A 10-year bond with a 5 percent coupon trading at par has a duration of approximately 7.8 years. A one-percentage-point rise in yield therefore reduces its price by roughly 7.8 percent, from $1,000 to about $922. A 30-year bond of the same coupon has a duration near 15 years, so the same rate increase reduces its price by approximately 15 percent. This sensitivity accounts for actual events: when central banks raised rates sharply in 2022, long-term bond funds — frequently regarded as the conservative component of a portfolio — declined 20 percent or more, precisely because their long duration made them acutely sensitive. Freedom from default is not freedom from price fluctuation.

Two kinds of bond risk

Default risk is the possibility that the issuer fails to pay — minimal for major governments, greater for financially weak companies, and compensated by a higher yield. Interest-rate risk is the possibility that rates move and reprice a bond before maturity, and it is present even for perfectly creditworthy government bonds. A U.S. Treasury carries almost no default risk but substantial interest-rate risk.

The yield curve

At any moment, bonds of the same credit quality but different maturities trade at different yields. The yield curve, or term structure of interest rates, plots yield against maturity. Its usual shape is upward-sloping, or normal: longer maturities carry higher yields, compensating investors for greater interest-rate risk and for uncertainty about future inflation. On occasion the curve inverts, with short-term yields exceeding long-term yields, when investors expect the central bank to reduce rates in response to a weakening economy. The graph contrasts the two shapes.

The slope of the curve carries information. An inverted yield curve has preceded most U.S. recessions of the past half century, because it reflects a market expectation that rates — and with them economic activity — will fall. The curve is therefore read not only as a menu of borrowing costs across maturities but as a summary of the market’s collective expectation about the path of the economy.

Credit risk, ratings, and spreads

A government bond and a corporate bond of identical maturity do not carry identical yields, because they differ in default risk. Rating agencies classify issuers on a scale from investment grade (the most creditworthy) down to speculative grade, informally termed high-yield or junk. The additional yield a borrower must offer above the default-free government bond of the same maturity is the credit spread, and it compensates investors for bearing default risk.

Worked example
A Northwind corporate bond

Suppose the 10-year Treasury yields 4.0 percent, and Northwind Components, rated investment grade but below the government, must offer a credit spread of 1.6 percent to attract buyers for a 10-year bond. Its yield to maturity is then $4.0\% + 1.6\% = 5.6\%$. Pricing a 10-year, $1,000, 5 percent coupon Northwind bond at a 5.6 percent yield gives:

Price = 50 × [1 − 1.056−10]/0.056 + 1000/1.05610 = 375.1 + 579.9 = $954.99

The bond trades at a discount to its $1,000 face value because its 5 percent coupon is below the 5.6 percent yield that its credit quality and the market require. Were Northwind’s credit to deteriorate, its spread would widen, its yield would rise, and the price of its outstanding bonds would fall — default risk and interest-rate risk acting together.

Exercise 5.4 Medium

A 10-year bond trading at par has a duration of about 7.8 years. Estimate the percentage and dollar change in its price if the market yield rises from 5% to 6%.

Solution

A 1-percentage-point rise in yield changes the price by approximately −duration × Δyield = −7.8 × 1% = −7.8%. On a $1,000 par price, that is a fall of about $78, to roughly $922.

The duration estimate is a linear approximation; because the true price-yield relation is convex, the actual price is slightly higher than $922. The approximation is accurate for small yield changes and understates the price for large ones.

Exercise 5.5 Medium

The 10-year Treasury yields 4.2%. Northwind’s 10-year bond yields 5.9%. (a) What is the credit spread? (b) If Northwind is downgraded and its spread widens to 2.5%, what is its new yield, and what happens to the price of its outstanding bonds?

Solution

(a) Spread = 5.9% − 4.2% = 1.7%.

(b) New yield = 4.2% + 2.5% = 6.7%. A higher required yield discounts the same fixed cash flows more heavily, so the price of Northwind’s outstanding bonds falls. A downgrade thus imposes a capital loss on existing holders even before any default occurs.

Exercise 5.6 Hard

An investor holds a 20-year bond and expects the central bank to cut rates sharply next year. Should this be welcomed or regretted, and what would happen to the bond’s price? How would the answer differ for a 1-year bond?

Solution

Welcomed. Falling rates raise bond prices, and the 20-year bond has long duration, so its price would rise substantially; the investor could sell at a gain or continue to hold an above-market coupon.

A 1-year bond would gain very little, because it matures almost immediately and has little future cash flow to reprice. The long bond is, in effect, a leveraged position on falling rates — advantageous if the forecast is correct and costly if rates instead rise. The choice of maturity is how a bond investor expresses a view on the direction of rates.

Where this came from

Government borrowing through tradable bonds dates to the Italian city-states of the Renaissance and to the funding of the Bank of England in 1694, which established a liquid market in government debt. The concept of duration was introduced by the Canadian economist Frederick Macaulay in 1938 as a measure of a bond’s effective maturity, and it became central to risk management as interest rates grew more volatile in the later twentieth century. The modern bond market, now exceeding one hundred trillion dollars worldwide, applies the same present-value pricing that this chapter develops.

Valuing a Company

📖 32 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A bond tells you its cash flows; a company makes you guess them. That gap is the central difficulty in valuing a business, and the source of its analytical interest. The machinery, though, is the one you already own: a company is worth the present value of the cash it will hand its owners over its lifetime. This chapter introduces the apparatus a professional analyst uses to estimate that figure: the financial statements that record what a firm has done, the ratios that convert those statements into judgment, and three valuation methods that translate the result into a price per share.

In 2000, the market valued a profitless pet-supply website at more than a billion dollars; eighteen months later it was worth nothing. In 2004, a search company went public at a price many analysts called indefensible; two decades later that price appears, in hindsight, conspicuously low. Both numbers were claims about future cash, and both were produced by serious people using the same tools you are about to learn. The tools did not fail. What separated the two outcomes was the quality of the assumptions fed into them. A valuation model does not remove uncertainty about the future; it forces you to state your assumptions in the open, where they can be examined, defended, and—when necessary—proven wrong for a reason rather than by accident.

A share is a claim on future cash

When you buy a share, you buy a fractional claim on every dollar the company will ever distribute to its owners: dividends paid along the way, plus whatever a buyer will one day pay for the share, which is itself a claim on the dividends still to come. Carried to its conclusion, the terminal sale price drops out of the arithmetic, leaving a precise statement of fundamental value: a stock is worth the present value of the future cash it returns to its owner. This is the principle of Chapter 2 applied to the most difficult asset in the book. The three valuation methods below do not disagree about this principle. They differ only in how they estimate the cash and what rate they discount it at.

Before any of them can be used, though, you need to read the record of what the company has actually done. That record is the set of financial statements, and learning to read them is a step that introductory treatments often compress. It is treated here deliberately.

The three financial statements

A public company reports its activity in three linked statements. Each answers a different question, and valuation draws on all three.

The income statement
Did the firm earn a profit over the period?

A flow, measured over a quarter or a year. It begins with revenue, subtracts the cost of goods sold to reach gross profit, subtracts operating expenses to reach operating income (often called EBIT, earnings before interest and taxes), then subtracts interest and taxes to reach net income, the “bottom line” that belongs to shareholders.

The balance sheet
What does the firm own and owe at a moment in time?

A snapshot, taken on the last day of the period. It obeys one identity: assets = liabilities + shareholders’ equity. Everything the firm controls is financed either by someone it owes (a liability) or by its owners (equity). Equity is therefore a residual: what would be left for owners if every asset were sold at its book value and every debt repaid.

The statement of cash flows
Where did the actual cash come from and go?

Net income is an accounting figure, shaped by judgment calls about depreciation, accruals, and timing. The cash-flow statement strips those away and tracks real cash across three activities: operating, investing, and financing. It is the bridge from reported profit to the free cash flow that valuation ultimately runs on.

Why profit is not cash

A firm can report a healthy profit and still be unable to pay its suppliers, because a sale booked on credit raises net income the day it is made but brings in no cash until the customer pays. The reverse happens too: a large upfront investment drains cash without touching that year’s profit, because the cost is spread over the asset’s life as depreciation. Valuation depends on cash, not accounting profit, and the gap between the two is a frequent source of analytical error.

Financial ratios: turning statements into judgment

Raw statement figures are hard to interpret on their own. Is $105m of net income good? The question has no answer until you ask “relative to what?” — relative to the revenue that produced it, the assets employed to earn it, or the equity the owners committed. A financial ratio is exactly that comparison: one statement figure divided by another, chosen so the result means something. Ratios are the vocabulary analysts use to compare a firm with its own past, with its competitors, and with the price the market is asking. They fall into five families.

Profitability: how much of each dollar is kept

Profitability ratios measure how efficiently a firm converts activity into earnings. The margin ratios are drawn directly from successive lines of the income statement: gross, operating, and net margin each divide a profit line by revenue. For Northwind, gross margin is $420/1{,}200 = 35\%$, operating margin is $170/1{,}200 \approx 14.2\%$, and net margin is $105/1{,}200 \approx 8.8\%$. Two return ratios go further by comparing profit to the capital used to generate it: return on assets (ROA = net income / total assets = $105/1{,}000 = 10.5\%$) and return on equity (ROE = net income / equity = $105/500 = 21\%$).

The gap between ROA and ROE is not incidental; it reflects the firm’s use of leverage, and the DuPont identity makes that mechanism explicit by decomposing ROE into three drivers:

The DuPont decomposition of ROE
ROE = (Net income / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
// net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier

For Northwind: $0.088 \times 1.20 \times 2.0 = 0.21$, which is the 21% ROE exactly. The decomposition tells you why a firm earns what it earns. A high ROE built on a wide net margin (a luxury brand) differs materially from one built on rapid asset turnover (a discount grocer) or one built on substantial borrowing (the equity multiplier of 2.0 indicates that half of Northwind’s assets are debt-financed). Two firms can report an identical 21% ROE while facing entirely different risks, and the DuPont decomposition is the means of distinguishing them.

Liquidity: can the firm pay its near-term bills?

Liquidity ratios ask whether short-term assets cover short-term obligations. The current ratio is current assets over current liabilities: $400/200 = 2.0$, meaning Northwind has two dollars of near-term assets for every dollar of near-term debt. The quick ratio (or acid test) strips out inventory, which can be slow to sell: $(400-160)/200 = 1.2$. The cash ratio, strictest of all, counts only cash: $90/200 = 0.45$. A higher ratio indicates greater safety, but a current ratio well above the industry norm may also indicate idle cash or excess inventory that the firm has not deployed productively.

Leverage and solvency: how much debt, and can it be serviced?

Where liquidity is about next month, solvency is about survival. The debt-to-equity ratio compares borrowed money to owners’ money; using interest-bearing debt, Northwind’s is $(80+300)/500 = 0.76$. More revealing is the interest coverage ratio (times interest earned), which asks how comfortably operating income covers the interest bill: $170/30 \approx 5.7\times$. A coverage of 5.7 means earnings could fall substantially before the firm struggled to pay its lenders; a coverage near 1.0 is a warning. Lenders and credit-rating agencies watch net debt to EBITDA closely: here net debt is total debt minus cash, $380-90=290$, so net-debt/EBITDA is $290/230 \approx 1.3\times$, a conservative figure.

Efficiency: how productively are the assets used?

Efficiency (or activity) ratios measure how quickly a firm turns its assets into sales and cash. Asset turnover is revenue over assets: $1{,}200/1{,}000 = 1.2$, the same figure that appeared in DuPont. Three cycle ratios track working capital. Days inventory is $365 \div (780/160) \approx 75$ days; days sales outstanding (how long customers take to pay) is $365 \div (1{,}200/150) \approx 46$ days; days payable (how long the firm takes to pay suppliers) is $365 \div (780/120) \approx 56$ days. Combine them into the cash conversion cycle — days inventory plus days receivable minus days payable, here $75 + 46 - 56 = 65$ days — the number of days the firm’s own cash is tied up before a sale finally returns it. A shorter cycle is preferable; a negative cycle, which some large retailers achieve, means that suppliers effectively finance the firm’s operations.

Valuation multiples: the price the market assigns

The first four families describe the business. The fifth compares the business to its price, and these are the ratios that connect statement analysis to the valuation methods that follow. The price-to-earnings ratio is price over earnings per share. Northwind’s EPS is $105/60 = \$1.75$, so its P/E is $28/1.75 = 16$: investors pay $16 for each dollar of current annual earnings. The price-to-book ratio compares price to accounting net worth per share ($500/60 = \$8.33$), giving $28/8.33 \approx 3.4$. The dividend yield is dividend per share over price, $0.70/28 = 2.5\%$.

Equity multiples like P/E are distorted by how a firm is financed, because earnings are measured after interest. To compare firms with different debt loads on equal footing, analysts turn to enterprise value — the value of the whole business to all its funders — defined as market capitalization plus net debt: $1{,}680 + 290 = \$1{,}970m$. The headline multiple is EV/EBITDA, here $1{,}970/230 \approx 8.6\times$, which is capital-structure neutral and therefore the standard tool for comparing acquisition targets.

No ratio is meaningful in isolation. Ratios should be used to form questions — why a margin is declining, why a firm trades below its peers — which the analyst then investigates.

Method 1: the dividend discount model

With the statements read, we can value the cash they imply. The simplest case is a firm that returns cash directly and predictably. If it pays a dividend that grows at a steady rate $g$ forever, its stock is a growing perpetuity, and the growing-perpetuity formula from Chapter 4 collapses into the elegant Gordon growth model:

Gordon growth (dividend discount) model
Price = D1 / (r − g)   // D₁ = next year's dividend, r = required return, g = growth rate

A stock about to pay a $2 dividend, growing 4% a year, held by investors who require a 9% return, is worth $2/(0.09-0.04) = \$40$. The formula holds only when $r > g$: a company that grew faster than its discount rate forever would be worth infinity, which no company is. Method 2: discounted cash flow (DCF)

Most interesting companies do not pay steady dividends; they reinvest to grow, and their payout today understates their value. For these we value the whole business by its free cash flow — the cash left after operating costs, taxes, and the investment needed to sustain growth. A DCF has three steps: project free cash flow explicitly for several years, capture everything after the forecast horizon in a single terminal value (usually a growing perpetuity), and discount both back at the company’s cost of capital, which Chapter 9 derives. Subtract net debt from the resulting enterprise value, divide by shares outstanding, and you have an intrinsic value per share to set against the market price.

A compact illustration makes the shape concrete. Suppose a firm’s free cash flow next year is $100m, growing 8% for five years, after which it settles to 3% growth forever; the cost of capital is 10%. The five explicit cash flows are discounted one by one. The terminal value, computed at the end of year five as $FCF_6/(r-g) = FCF_6/(0.10-0.03)$, is then itself discounted back five years. In a realistic model that terminal value often accounts for sixty to eighty percent of the total — which is precisely why DCF demands discipline.

DCF is the principal tool of professional valuation, and it is the model you will build in the arena. Its power is also its hazard: the answer depends on a discount rate and a long-run growth rate that can only be estimated, and the terminal value magnifies both. A careful analyst never reports a single number; they run the model across a range of assumptions and report a valuation band.

Method 3: multiples, the quick comparison

When a rapid check is wanted rather than a full model, the valuation multiples from the ratio dashboard serve that purpose. Multiples value a company by comparison: if similar firms trade at 20 times earnings (a P/E of 20) and this firm earns $5 per share, a first estimate of its value is $20 \times 5 = \$100$ per share. The same logic runs on EV/EBITDA for the whole firm, on price-to-book for banks, on EV/sales for companies not yet profitable. Multiples are rapid, market-based, and easily communicated, but they embed the market’s prevailing sentiment toward the entire sector. “Fairly valued relative to other overpriced stocks” is a genuine hazard, which is why a multiple serves as a cross-check rather than a conclusion.

The same idea, three lenses

Dividends, discounted free cash flow, and multiples are three windows onto one question: what is a claim on this company’s future cash worth today? A careful analyst triangulates. When a DCF, a dividend model, and comparable multiples all cluster near $80, that agreement is far stronger than any single method alone. When they diverge sharply, the disagreement is itself the most useful output, because it points straight to the one assumption the whole valuation turns on.

Solution

DuPont: net margin 105/1,200 = 8.75%, asset turnover 1,200/1,000 = 1.2, equity multiplier 1,000/500 = 2.0; product 0.0875 × 1.2 × 2.0 = 21%.

Repaying all debt with equity removes the interest expense (raising net income toward 170 × (1−0.25) = $127.5m) but also drives the equity multiplier toward 1.0, because assets would now be financed almost entirely by owners. ROE would fall substantially — leverage was doing much of the lifting.

A lower ROE here is not a worse outcome. By shedding debt the firm also sheds financial risk: interest no longer has first claim on operating income, and the chance of distress in a downturn falls. The lesson is that ROE can be raised simply by borrowing more, so a high ROE must always be read alongside the leverage that produced it. This is precisely why DuPont, not ROE alone, is the right lens.

Exercise 6.6 Hard

Two analysts value the same company. Analyst A assumes 3% terminal growth and a 9% discount rate; Analyst B assumes 5% growth and an 8% discount rate. Explain why their valuations could differ enormously, and what that tells you about how DCF results should be reported.

Solution

The terminal value scales roughly with 1/(r−g). Analyst A’s gap is 0.09−0.03 = 0.06; Analyst B’s is 0.08−0.05 = 0.03. B’s terminal value is therefore about twice A’s before any other difference, and since terminal value is usually most of the total, B’s valuation could plausibly be double A’s.

The result is exquisitely sensitive to two of the least certain inputs in the whole model. This is not a flaw to conceal but a fact to manage: run the model across a range of discount and growth assumptions and report a valuation band, never a single false-precision figure. The arena is built to let you feel this sensitivity directly.

Where this came from

Ratio analysis grew out of lending. In the early twentieth century, bankers deciding whether to extend credit developed the current ratio and interest coverage as quick tests of whether a borrower could repay. The DuPont Company’s own finance staff built the ROE decomposition in the 1910s to manage a sprawling industrial empire, and it still bears the firm’s name. Benjamin Graham and David Dodd turned ratios into a philosophy of investing in Security Analysis (1934), insisting that price be judged against demonstrable value. Discounted cash flow, though its roots reach back to compound-interest tables, became the standard corporate tool only after the mathematics of the cost of capital was worked out in the 1950s and 1960s. Each method answers the same old question — what is future cash worth now — with the instruments of its era.

Investment Decisions: NPV & IRR

📖 30 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A company confronts the same question many times each year: should it commit capital to a given project? Whether to build a factory, launch a product, or acquire a competitor is, in every case, an investment decision. Finance provides one correct criterion for such decisions — the net present value rule — together with several widely used alternatives that usually agree with it and occasionally fail. This chapter develops each method and establishes which to rely upon when they conflict.

A manager presents two projects. Project A recovers its cost in two years; Project B requires four. On the basis that A returns the investment sooner, the manager recommends A. Over their full lives, however, B generates considerably more value. Selecting A because it recovers its cost sooner would forgo that additional value. The methods developed in this chapter exist to prevent exactly this error, by comparing projects according to the value they create rather than the speed with which they return their cost.

What counts as a cash flow

Before any rule is applied, the correct cash flows must be identified. The relevant quantity is the incremental cash flow: the difference the project makes to the firm’s total cash, and nothing else. Two distinctions follow from this definition. A sunk cost — money already spent and unrecoverable, such as a completed market study — is excluded, because it does not change with the decision. An opportunity cost — the value of a resource in its next-best use, such as land the firm already owns and could otherwise sell — is included, because using it for the project forgoes that alternative. Applying a correct rule to the wrong cash flows produces the wrong decision, so the identification of incremental cash flows precedes the arithmetic.

The net present value rule

Net present value was introduced in Chapter 2; here it becomes the central rule of corporate finance. A project’s net present value (NPV) is the present value of all the cash it generates, less its cost today:

The decision rule
NPV = −C₀ + Σ Ct / (1+r)t    // Accept if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0

The reasoning is exact. A positive NPV means the project is worth more than it costs, so accepting it increases the owners’ wealth by precisely that amount in today’s dollars; a negative NPV reduces it. Among competing projects, the one with the highest NPV is preferred. No rule is more fundamental, because NPV measures directly the quantity a firm exists to maximise: the wealth of its owners. The discount rate $r$ is the project’s hurdle rate — the required return on capital of comparable risk, constructed as the cost of capital in Chapter 9. Internal rate of return and the NPV profile

Many managers prefer a single percentage that can be compared against a threshold. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which a project’s NPV equals zero — in effect, the return the project earns on the capital invested in it. The IRR rule accepts a project when its IRR exceeds the hurdle rate. A project with a 15 percent IRR clears a 10 percent hurdle and is accepted.

The relationship between NPV and the discount rate is made explicit by the NPV profile, which plots a project’s NPV against the discount rate. The point at which the profile crosses zero is the IRR. The graph shows the profiles of two projects, each costing $50,000: Project A, whose cash flows are concentrated in the early years, and Project B, whose cash flows are larger but arrive later.

Both profiles slope downward, since a higher discount rate reduces the present value of future cash. Project A crosses zero at an IRR of 20.0 percent and Project B at 18.7 percent, so the IRR rule ranks A above B. Yet the profiles cross at a discount rate of about 15.0 percent — the Fisher crossover — and below that rate Project B has the higher NPV. At a 10 percent hurdle, Project B creates $14,492 of value against Project A’s $12,092, so B is the better project even though A has the higher IRR. This is the central limitation of IRR: because it is a rate rather than an amount, it can rank a smaller, earlier project above a larger, more valuable one.

IRR carries two further difficulties. When a project’s cash flows change sign more than once — for instance, a large clean-up cost in a final year — there can be multiple internal rates of return, none of them meaningful. And the measure implicitly assumes that interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself, which overstates the return when the IRR is high. For these reasons, when the IRR rule and the NPV rule disagree, the NPV rule governs, because it measures value directly in dollars.

Payback, discounted payback, and the profitability index

The payback period is the time a project takes to recover its initial cost. It is simple and widely used, but it has two defects: it ignores the time value of money, counting a dollar in year three equally with a dollar in year one, and it ignores every cash flow after the cutoff, so it cannot distinguish a project that earns a great deal after payback from one that earns nothing. The discounted payback period repairs the first defect by discounting the cash flows before accumulating them, but it still ignores everything beyond the cutoff. Both measures are useful as checks on how long capital is committed, not as criteria for value.

The profitability index (PI) addresses the scale limitation of raw dollar NPV. It is the present value of a project’s future cash flows divided by its initial cost; a project is acceptable when the index exceeds one, which is equivalent to a positive NPV. The index measures value created per dollar invested, which makes it useful under capital rationing — when limited funds must be allocated among several acceptable projects, those with the highest index are preferred, subject to the budget.

The hierarchy of rules

NPV is the primary criterion: it measures value in dollars and does not mislead. IRR is a useful complement that usually agrees with NPV but can fail on scale, sign changes, and the reinvestment assumption. The profitability index ranks projects by value per dollar when capital is constrained. Payback and discounted payback are checks on the commitment of capital, not measures of value. When the criteria conflict, NPV governs.

Exercise 7.4 Medium

A firm spent $40,000 last year on a market study for a product. It owns a warehouse it could rent out for $25,000 a year, but would instead use it for the product. Which of these figures belongs in the product’s NPV analysis, and which does not? Explain.

Solution

The $40,000 market study is a sunk cost: it has been spent and cannot be recovered, and it does not change with the decision, so it is excluded.

The $25,000 of forgone rent is an opportunity cost: using the warehouse for the product gives up the rental income, so $25,000 a year is a genuine cost of the project and is included. The rule is to count only incremental cash flows — what changes because the project is undertaken.

Exercise 7.5 Hard

Northwind’s machine (cost $120,000; $35,000 per year for five years, with $10,000 salvage in year 5) is evaluated at a 10% hurdle. Its NPV is about $18,900. Explain what happens to this decision if the hurdle rate rises to 15%, and relate your answer to the NPV profile.

Solution

At 15%, each future cash flow is discounted more heavily, so the NPV falls. The project’s IRR is about 15.8 percent; as long as the hurdle remains below that figure, the NPV stays positive and the project is accepted, but by a smaller margin. On the NPV profile, raising the hurdle moves leftward-to-rightward along the downward-sloping curve toward the IRR, where the NPV reaches zero.

Were the hurdle to exceed the IRR, the NPV would turn negative and the project would be rejected. The IRR is precisely the hurdle rate at which the decision changes.

Exercise 7.6 Hard

A colleague argues that a project with a 2-year payback and a 30% IRR should be accepted without further analysis. Give two reasons this reasoning can be mistaken.

Solution

Payback is blind to later cash flows. Recovering the cost in two years says nothing about years three onward; a large decommissioning cost or negative later cash flows could destroy value that a fast payback conceals and only NPV would reveal.

IRR can be unreliable. If the cash flows change sign more than once, the IRR may not be unique; and a 30 percent IRR on a small project may create less value than a lower IRR on a larger one. The dependable procedure is to compute the NPV at the correct hurdle rate and decide on that basis.

Where this came from

The net present value rule follows from Irving Fisher’s 1930 argument that an asset is worth the present value of the income it produces; the discount rate at which two projects’ present values are equal is still called the Fisher crossover in his honour. The internal rate of return was advanced by John Maynard Keynes, as the “marginal efficiency of capital,” and by the economist Kenneth Boulding in the 1930s. The systematic use of discounted-cash-flow analysis in corporate capital budgeting spread through American industry in the 1950s, and it remains the standard framework taught and practised today.

Risk & Return

📖 30 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Earlier chapters discounted cash flows as though they were known with certainty. They are not. The future is uncertain, and the central result of finance regarding uncertainty is that a portion of risk can be eliminated at no cost, by combining assets whose fortunes do not move together. This chapter defines risk and return precisely, establishes why diversification reduces risk without reducing expected return, and develops the portfolio theory constructed by hand in the companion arena.

Consider two businesses in one town: a seller of umbrellas and a seller of ice cream. Each faces an uncertain year, since each depends almost entirely on the weather. Held alone, either is a volatile proposition. Held together, the outcome changes markedly: wet months favour umbrellas while dry months favour ice cream, so the combined income is comparatively stable although each business swings widely on its own. The risk of neither business was reduced; the combination offset them, by pairing risks that move in opposite directions. This is diversification, and it is the foundational idea of portfolio theory.

Measuring return and risk

Reasoning about uncertainty requires measurement. The expected return of an investment is the probability-weighted average of its possible returns — the single best estimate of the outcome. Risk is the extent to which the actual return may depart from that average, measured by the variance, the probability-weighted average of the squared deviations from the mean, or by its square root, the standard deviation, commonly termed volatility. A high standard deviation indicates a wide range of outcomes; a low one indicates outcomes clustered near the average.

Worked example
Expected return and standard deviation

A share of Northwind Components will return +30 percent in a strong economy (probability 0.5) and −10 percent in a weak economy (probability 0.5).

E[R] = 0.5(30%) + 0.5(−10%) = +10%

Variance = 0.5(30−10)2 + 0.5(−10−10)2 = 0.5(400) + 0.5(400) = 400, so the standard deviation is √400 = 20%.

The expected return is 10 percent, but the realised return is never 10 percent; it is either +30 or −10. The 20-percentage-point standard deviation measures that dispersion, and it is the risk of holding the share in isolation.

Diversification

When assets are combined into a portfolio, the portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of the individual expected returns, each weight being the fraction of capital held in that asset. The portfolio’s risk, however, is generally less than the weighted average of the individual risks, and can be considerably less when the assets do not move together. The quantity that governs this is the correlation, a measure of how closely two assets’ returns move together, ranging from +1 (identical movement) through 0 (unrelated) to −1 (exactly opposite movement). The related covariance is the unscaled form of the same relationship, and it appears directly in the portfolio variance formula.

Variance of a two-asset portfolio
σp2 = wA2σA2 + wB2σB2 + 2 wAwB ρAB σAσB   // ρ = correlation; the last term is where diversification acts

The final term carries the correlation. When the correlation is below +1, that term is smaller than it would otherwise be, so the portfolio variance falls below the weighted average of the individual variances. At a correlation of +1 there is no reduction, because the assets move in lockstep. The graph plots the risk-return combinations available from two assets — one with a 10 percent return and 18 percent volatility, the other with 6 percent and 8 percent — as the weights vary, for three correlations.

At a correlation of +1 the combinations lie on a straight line between the two assets; as the correlation falls, the curve bows to the left, meaning that for a given expected return the portfolio achieves a lower risk. At a correlation of −0.5, the lowest-risk combination has a standard deviation of about 5.4 percent — below that of either asset individually — achieved at roughly a 26 percent weight in the riskier asset. This leftward curvature is the reduction of risk through diversification, obtained without any reduction in expected return.

How many stocks are enough? Systematic and unsystematic risk

Diversification has a limit, and the limit is instructive. As more stocks are added to a portfolio, the risk declines, rapidly at first and then more slowly, approaching a floor that further diversification cannot penetrate. The graph shows this for a representative case, with single-stock volatility of 40 percent falling toward a floor of about 20 percent.

The decline and the floor correspond to the two components of a stock’s total risk. Unsystematic risk, also called specific or idiosyncratic risk, is unique to an individual company — a product failure, a lawsuit, the loss of a key customer. Because such events are independent across companies, their effects offset one another in a large portfolio, so this component is diversified away; it is the portion of the curve that falls as stocks are added. Systematic risk, also called market risk, is common to all companies — recessions, interest-rate movements, broad shifts in sentiment — and it cannot be diversified away, because it affects every holding at once; it is the floor the curve approaches. Most of the reduction is achieved with twenty to thirty well-chosen stocks.

The risk that is rewarded

Since unsystematic risk can be eliminated at no cost through diversification, the market offers no compensation for bearing it: any investor can remove it. The only risk that earns a premium is systematic risk, which cannot be escaped. This result is the basis of the Capital Asset Pricing Model in Chapter 9: an asset’s expected return depends only on its systematic risk, measured by a coefficient called beta. An investor holding a diversified portfolio is compensated for precisely the risk that cannot be avoided.

The efficient frontier and risk-adjusted return

Extending the two-asset analysis to many assets produces a region of attainable risk-return combinations whose upper-left boundary is the efficient frontier: the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk. A rational investor selects a portfolio on this frontier; any portfolio below it is dominated by one on the frontier with the same risk and a higher return. The leftmost point of the frontier is the minimum-variance portfolio, the combination with the lowest attainable risk.

To compare portfolios of differing risk on a common basis, the Sharpe ratio divides a portfolio’s return in excess of the risk-free rate by its standard deviation. A portfolio returning 10 percent with a 15 percent standard deviation, when the risk-free rate is 3 percent, has a Sharpe ratio of $(10-3)/15 = 0.47$: 0.47 percentage points of excess return per unit of risk. A higher Sharpe ratio indicates a more favourable trade-off, and the portfolio on the efficient frontier with the highest Sharpe ratio is the preferred risky portfolio under the theory developed in Chapter 9.

In the two-asset sheet, set both assets to the same volatility and expected return, then move the correlation from +1 to −1. Describe the effect on portfolio risk and explain it.

This is diversification in its purest form: the expected return is unchanged while the risk falls as the assets become less correlated. Real assets seldom reach −1, but even modest positive correlations leave substantial risk to diversify.

Exercise 8.5 Hard

An investor holds a single stock and argues: “It is risky, so I expect a high return to compensate.” Using the systematic/unsystematic distinction, explain why the market may not reward much of that stock’s risk, and what the investor should do.

Solution

Much of a single stock’s total risk is unsystematic — company-specific events that a diversified holding would offset. The market compensates only systematic risk, the part no investor can escape. The holder of a single stock therefore bears substantial risk (the specific component) for no additional expected return.

The remedy is to diversify. A broad portfolio sheds the unrewarded specific risk, leaving the investor exposed to, and compensated for, only systematic risk — the same expected return at far lower total risk. This is the rationale for index funds and the basis of Chapter 9’s pricing of assets by systematic risk alone.

Exercise 8.6 Hard

From 20 to 30 stocks it falls only from about 21.4 to 21.0 percent, because most of the diversifiable risk has already been removed.

The risk remaining at 30 stocks, about 20 percent, is systematic (market) risk, which affects all stocks together and cannot be diversified away regardless of how many are added. It is the risk for which the market pays a premium.

Where this came from

The mathematical treatment of diversification was introduced by Harry Markowitz in a 1952 paper, “Portfolio Selection,” which showed that an investor should care about the risk and return of a portfolio as a whole rather than of each asset separately, and defined the efficient frontier. The work earned Markowitz the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics, shared with William Sharpe, whose eponymous ratio and Capital Asset Pricing Model extended the framework to the pricing of individual assets — the subject of Chapter 9.

Cost of Capital & Capital Structure

📖 30 min read
🧮 2 live spreadsheets
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Every discount rate used in earlier chapters was taken as given. This chapter derives it. A company’s capital is not free: both shareholders and lenders require a return, and the blended cost of that capital — the weighted average cost of capital, or WACC — is the hurdle rate every project must clear. The chapter also addresses one of the most debated questions in corporate finance: whether the proportion of debt and equity a firm uses affects its value.

Consider two otherwise identical companies, with the same assets and the same operating profits. One is financed entirely by shareholders; the other borrows half of its capital. Are they worth the same amount? In 1958, Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller demonstrated that, in a world without taxes or bankruptcy costs, the two are worth exactly the same, because the method of financing does not alter the total cash the assets produce. The result earned both economists Nobel Prizes. It does not settle the debt-versus-equity question, however, because the actual economy contains taxes and the possibility of financial distress, and those departures from the ideal determine the answer.

The cost of equity: the CAPM

What return do shareholders require? Because they could invest their capital elsewhere, they demand at least the risk-free rate together with a premium for the systematic risk they bear, as established in Chapter 8. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) states this precisely: an asset’s required return depends on a single measure of risk, its beta, which quantifies how strongly the asset’s return moves with the return on the overall market.

The CAPM
Required return = rf + β × (market risk premium)   // rf = risk-free rate; β = systematic risk

A beta of 1 indicates that the stock moves with the market; a beta of 1.5 indicates movements 50 percent larger than the market’s, implying greater systematic risk and a higher required return; a beta of 0.6 indicates a steadier stock. The market risk premium is the additional return that stocks have historically provided over the risk-free rate, approximately 5 to 6 percent. Beta is estimated empirically, as the slope of a regression of the stock’s returns on the market’s returns. The model contains no term for a company’s specific risk, consistent with Chapter 8: only systematic risk, captured by beta, is priced. The relationship is displayed by the Security Market Line, which plots required return against beta.

The line begins at the risk-free rate, where beta is zero, and rises with beta at a slope equal to the market risk premium. Every correctly priced asset lies on this line; the market portfolio sits at a beta of one. A share of Northwind Components, with a beta of 1.1, a 4 percent risk-free rate, and a 5.5 percent market risk premium, has a required return of $4\% + 1.1 \times 5.5\% = 10.05\%$. This is the firm’s cost of equity — the return its shareholders require and, therefore, the discount rate applied to its equity cash flows.

The cost of debt and the tax shield

Debt is less costly than equity for two reasons. First, lenders bear less risk than shareholders — they are paid before shareholders and hold contractual claims — so they require a lower return, the interest rate on the firm’s borrowing. Second, interest payments are tax-deductible: they reduce taxable income, so the government bears part of the cost. A firm paying 6 percent interest in a 25 percent tax bracket has an after-tax cost of debt of $6\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 4.5\%$. This reduction, the tax shield, is a principal reason firms use debt. In practice the cost of debt is estimated from the yield on the firm’s own bonds, the measure developed in Chapter 5.

Combining the two: the WACC

A firm financed by both debt and equity has a blended cost of capital, the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), equal to the cost of each source weighted by its share of total financing, measured at market values:

WACC
WACC = (E/V) × requity  +  (D/V) × rdebt × (1 − tax)   // E = equity, D = debt, V = E + D
Worked example
Northwind’s WACC

Northwind has a market capitalisation of $1,680m in equity and $380m in debt, so total financing is $2,060m. Its cost of equity, from the CAPM above, is 10.05 percent; the yield on its bonds is 5.6 percent (Chapter 5); and its tax rate is 25 percent, giving an after-tax cost of debt of $5.6\% \times 0.75 = 4.2\%$.

WACC = (1680/2060)(10.05%) + (380/2060)(4.2%) = 8.19% + 0.77% = 9.0%

Northwind’s WACC is about 9 percent. This is the discount rate the firm applies to projects of average risk, and the hurdle rate used in the capital-budgeting decisions of Chapter 7: a project returning more than 9 percent creates value, and one returning less destroys it.

Capital structure: does the debt-equity mix matter?

Since debt is less costly than equity, one might ask why a firm does not finance itself almost entirely with debt and reduce its WACC accordingly. This is the capital structure question. The Modigliani–Miller theorem provides the starting point. Its first proposition states that, in a world without taxes or distress costs, firm value is independent of the financing mix, because financing does not change the cash the assets generate. Its second proposition explains the mechanism: as a firm adds debt, the risk borne by the remaining shareholders rises, so the cost of equity increases by exactly enough to offset the cheaper debt, leaving the WACC unchanged.

The actual economy departs from these conditions in two opposing ways. The tax shield makes debt genuinely valuable, which favours more borrowing. But a high level of debt raises the probability of financial distress — the costs associated with possible bankruptcy, including lost customers, strained supplier terms, forced asset sales, and higher interest demanded by lenders. The trade-off theory holds that the optimal capital structure balances the marginal tax benefit of additional debt against the rising expected costs of distress. The graph shows the resulting behaviour of the three costs as leverage increases.

The cost of equity rises steadily with leverage, as Modigliani–Miller’s second proposition predicts. The after-tax cost of debt is low and roughly constant at modest leverage, then rises as lenders begin to price in default risk. The WACC, their weighted average, falls at first as the tax shield dominates, reaches a minimum at a moderate level of debt — here about 40 percent of financing — and rises thereafter as distress costs take over. That minimum is the optimal capital structure. A competing account, the pecking-order theory, observes that firms prefer to finance first from retained earnings, then from debt, and only last from new equity, because issuing equity can signal that management considers the shares overvalued; this explains why profitable firms often use less debt than the trade-off theory alone would suggest.

Exercise 9.1 Easy

A firm pays 8% interest on its debt and faces a 30% tax rate. What is its after-tax cost of debt?

Solution

8% × (1 − 0.30) = 8% × 0.70 = 5.6%. The deductibility of interest reduces the effective cost from 8% to 5.6%; the government bears nearly a third of the interest expense.

Exercise 9.2 Easy

A stock has a beta of 0.8. The risk-free rate is 3.5% and the market risk premium is 5%. What is its cost of equity, and is it above or below the market’s required return?

Solution

3.5% + 0.8 × 5% = 3.5% + 4.0% = 7.5%.

The market portfolio (beta 1) requires 3.5% + 5% = 8.5%, so this stock’s required return is below the market’s, because its beta is below 1 — it carries less systematic risk.

Exercise 9.3 Medium

A firm is 70% equity, 30% debt. Cost of equity 12%, pre-tax cost of debt 6%, tax rate 25%. Compute its WACC.

Solution

After-tax cost of debt: 6% × 0.75 = 4.5%.

WACC = 0.70(12%) + 0.30(4.5%) = 8.4% + 1.35% = 9.75%.

Any project this firm considers must clear a 9.75% return to add value.

Exercise 9.4 Medium

Northwind (cost of equity 10.05%, after-tax cost of debt 4.2%, WACC about 9%) is considering a new product line judged riskier than its existing business, with a beta of 1.6. Should it discount the project’s cash flows at 9%? Explain.

Solution

No. The firm-wide WACC of 9% reflects the risk of Northwind’s average project. A riskier project requires a higher discount rate. Its cost of equity at beta 1.6 is 4% + 1.6 × 5.5% = 12.8%, well above the 10.05% used for the average business.

Discounting a high-risk project at the lower firm-wide WACC would overstate its NPV and could lead the firm to accept value-destroying projects. The correct practice is to use a project-specific discount rate that reflects the project’s own systematic risk.

Exercise 9.5 Hard

A student argues: “Since debt is cheaper than equity, a firm should finance itself with 99% debt to minimise its WACC.” Using both Modigliani–Miller and the trade-off theory, explain why this is mistaken.

Solution

Modigliani–Miller: in a frictionless setting, increasing debt raises the risk borne by the remaining equity, so the cost of equity rises by exactly enough to offset the cheaper debt, and the WACC does not fall. Value cannot be created merely by changing the financing mix.

Trade-off theory: the tax shield does make some debt valuable, so firms lean toward borrowing. But at 99 percent debt the expected costs of financial distress — bankruptcy, lost customers, forced asset sales, and the higher interest lenders demand once default becomes likely — rise steeply and eventually exceed the tax benefit, while the cost of debt itself climbs. The optimum is moderate leverage, which is why sound firms carry some debt but rarely approach such extremes.

Exercise 9.6 Hard

Using the WACC-versus-leverage graph, explain why the WACC first falls and then rises as debt increases, and relate the minimum point to the trade-off theory.

Solution

At low leverage, adding debt substitutes cheap, tax-advantaged financing for costly equity, so the WACC falls, even though the cost of equity is rising. The after-tax cost of debt is low and roughly constant in this region, so the tax shield dominates.

Beyond a moderate level, lenders begin to demand higher interest to compensate for default risk and the cost of equity rises more steeply, so the weighted average turns upward. The minimum of the curve — about 40 percent debt in the graph — is the point at which the marginal tax benefit of further debt just equals the marginal expected cost of distress. That balance is precisely the optimal capital structure of the trade-off theory.

Where this came from

The Capital Asset Pricing Model was developed independently by William Sharpe, John Lintner, and Jan Mossin in the early 1960s, building on Markowitz’s portfolio theory; Sharpe received the 1990 Nobel Prize for the work. The capital-structure propositions were established by Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller in 1958, with the tax extension following in 1963; both received Nobel Prizes, Modigliani in 1985 and Miller in 1990. The subsequent trade-off and pecking-order theories, developed through the 1970s and 1980s, reconciled the Modigliani–Miller framework with the financing patterns observed in practice.

Portfolios & Diversification

📖 30 min read
📈 2 figures
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

Chapter 8 established that combining imperfectly correlated assets reduces risk without a proportional sacrifice of expected return. This chapter develops that observation into a complete theory of portfolio selection. It shows that, among all attainable combinations of risky assets, a well-defined subset is efficient; that introducing a risk-free asset identifies a single optimal portfolio of risky assets that is common to every investor; and that, in equilibrium, this portfolio is the market as a whole. These results, due to Markowitz, Tobin, and Sharpe, supply the theoretical foundation for index investing and connect directly to the Capital Asset Pricing Model of Chapter 9.

In 1952 Harry Markowitz published a formal treatment of portfolio selection that moved the object of analysis from the individual security to the portfolio as a whole. His central argument was that an asset should be judged not by its own variability but by its contribution to the variability of the portfolio that holds it. Within a decade James Tobin showed that the availability of a risk-free asset separates the investment decision into two independent parts, and William Sharpe derived the equilibrium implication that the optimal risky portfolio must be the market portfolio. The practical consequence that followed from this body of theory, the low-cost index fund, reached ordinary investors only many years later.

The return and risk of a portfolio

A portfolio is a collection of assets held together, each with a portfolio weight — the fraction of total wealth invested in it, with the weights summing to one. The expected return of a portfolio is simply the weighted average of the expected returns of its holdings. For two assets A and B with weights $w_A$ and $w_B = 1 - w_A$, the expected portfolio return is $E[r_p] = w_A\,E[r_A] + w_B\,E[r_B]$. Risk, however, does not average in this way, and that distinction is the entire subject of this chapter.

The risk of a portfolio is measured by the variance (or its square root, the standard deviation) of its return. For two assets, the portfolio variance is

Variance of a two-asset portfolio
σp2 = wA2σA2 + wB2σB2 + 2 wAwBρABσAσB   // two own-variance terms plus a covariance term

where $\sigma_A$ and $\sigma_B$ are the standard deviations of the two assets and $\rho_{AB}$ is the correlation coefficient between their returns — a number between −1 and +1 that measures the degree to which the two returns move together (Chapter 8). The first two terms are the contributions of each asset in isolation; the third, the covariance term, captures how the two assets move relative to one another. Because the correlation is usually well below one, this third term is smaller than it would be if the assets moved in lockstep, and the portfolio standard deviation falls below the weighted average of the individual standard deviations. That reduction is diversification expressed algebraically.

Combining two assets

Consider a concrete pairing. Shares of Northwind Components, the manufacturer used throughout this book, have an expected return of 11 percent and a standard deviation of 25 percent. A broad bond fund has an expected return of 5 percent and a standard deviation of 10 percent. Their returns have a correlation of 0.10. The figure traces every combination of the two, from a position held entirely in bonds to one held entirely in Northwind shares.

The curve bows toward the upper left rather than following a straight line between the two assets. That curvature is the diversification effect: because the two returns are only weakly correlated, part of each asset’s variability is offset by the other, and intermediate combinations carry less risk than a simple average of the two standard deviations would imply. The lower the correlation, the more pronounced the curvature; had the correlation been negative, the leftward bulge would be larger still.

Worked example
A 50/50 Northwind-and-bond portfolio

Place half of the portfolio in Northwind shares and half in the bond fund. The expected return is the weighted average: E[r_p] = 0.5(11%) + 0.5(5%) = 8.0%.

The standard deviation is not the average. Applying the two-asset formula with $\sigma_A = 0.25$, $\sigma_B = 0.10$, and $\rho = 0.10$:

σ_p² = 0.5²(0.25²) + 0.5²(0.10²) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.10)(0.25)(0.10) = 0.019375

so σ_p = √0.019375 = 13.9%. The simple average of the two standard deviations is 0.5(25%) + 0.5(10%) = 17.5%. Combining the assets has removed about 3.6 percentage points of risk while the expected return remains the exact weighted average. The reduction in risk is obtained at no cost in expected return; this is the defining feature of diversification.

One combination carries less risk than any other. The minimum-variance portfolio is the mix with the smallest possible standard deviation. For two assets its weight in asset A is $w_A^{*} = \dfrac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B}$. For Northwind and the bond fund this evaluates to about 0.11, so the least-risky combination holds roughly 11 percent in Northwind shares and 89 percent in bonds, with a standard deviation of about 9.6 percent — below that of the bond fund held alone. Even an investor who wants the lowest attainable risk should hold some of the riskier asset, because a small allocation to shares that move differently from bonds reduces the variability of the whole.

The efficient frontier

With many risky assets rather than two, the set of attainable combinations fills a region in risk-return space. Its upper-left boundary is the efficient frontier: the set of portfolios that offer the highest expected return attainable at each level of risk, or equivalently the lowest risk attainable for each level of expected return. A portfolio lying strictly inside the region is said to be dominated — another portfolio offers more expected return for the same risk, or the same expected return for less risk — and no investor seeking return and avoiding risk should hold it. A rational investor therefore restricts attention to the frontier. The Portfolio Builder arena constructs this frontier from live data and allows the reader to locate positions on it.

Introducing a risk-free asset

Suppose the investor can also hold a risk-free asset — an instrument whose return is known in advance with certainty, such as a short-term Treasury bill (Chapter 5) — either by lending at its rate (buying the bill) or by borrowing at that rate. Combining the risk-free asset with any risky portfolio produces a straight line in risk-return space, because the risk-free asset has zero standard deviation and zero correlation with everything. The line that rises most steeply is the one drawn from the risk-free rate to the point where it is just tangent to the efficient frontier. This line is the capital allocation line, and the risky portfolio at the point of tangency is the tangency portfolio.

The slope of the capital allocation line is the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio — its expected return in excess of the risk-free rate, divided by its standard deviation. The Sharpe ratio measures reward per unit of risk, and the tangency portfolio is, by construction, the risky portfolio with the highest attainable Sharpe ratio. Every point on the capital allocation line has that same slope, so an investor who holds the tangency portfolio and adjusts risk by moving along the line obtains the best available reward-to-risk trade-off at every level of risk.

Definition
Sharpe ratio

The ratio of a portfolio’s expected excess return to its standard deviation, $S = (E[r_p] - r_f)\,/\,\sigma_p$. It expresses reward per unit of total risk. The tangency portfolio maximises this ratio; identifying it is the objective of the Portfolio Builder arena.

Worked example
Dialing risk up and down with the risk-free asset

Take a tangency portfolio with an expected return of 8.1 percent and a standard deviation of 12.8 percent, with the risk-free rate at 3 percent. Its Sharpe ratio is (8.1% − 3%) / 12.8% = 0.40.

An investor seeking less risk places 60 percent in the tangency portfolio and 40 percent in the risk-free asset. The expected return is 0.6(8.1%) + 0.4(3%) = 6.06% and the standard deviation is 0.6(12.8%) = 7.68%, because the risk-free portion contributes no variability. The Sharpe ratio is (6.06% − 3%) / 7.68% = 0.40 — unchanged.

An investor seeking more risk than the tangency portfolio offers borrows at the risk-free rate and invests the proceeds in the same portfolio. Placing 120 percent in the tangency portfolio and −20 percent in the risk-free asset (a 20 percent borrowing) gives an expected return of 1.2(8.1%) − 0.2(3%) = 9.12% and a standard deviation of 1.2(12.8%) = 15.36%. In every case the risky holdings are the same portfolio; only the split between it and the risk-free asset changes.

The separation theorem

The preceding example illustrates a general result known as the separation theorem, due to Tobin. The choice of the best risky portfolio is separate from, and independent of, the investor’s tolerance for risk. Every investor, regardless of how cautious or aggressive, should hold the same tangency portfolio of risky assets; they differ only in how they divide their wealth between that portfolio and the risk-free asset. A conservative investor holds more of the risk-free asset; an aggressive one borrows to hold more of the identical risky portfolio. Because only two building blocks are required — the risk-free asset and the tangency portfolio — the result is also called two-fund separation.

The market portfolio and index funds

If every investor holds the same tangency portfolio, then in market equilibrium that portfolio must contain every risky asset in proportion to its market value. If it did not — if some asset were held by no one — there would be no buyers for it at the prevailing price, and the price would adjust until the asset was held. The tangency portfolio is therefore the market portfolio: the value-weighted collection of all risky assets. This is the theoretical basis for the index fund, a fund that holds the constituents of a broad market index in their market-value proportions and seeks only to match the index rather than to outperform it. Holding the market portfolio at low cost is the practical implementation of the theory developed in this chapter.

The conclusion also closes the argument of Chapter 9. If the portfolio that every investor holds is the market portfolio, then the relevant measure of an individual asset’s risk is its contribution to the variance of that portfolio, which is its beta. The portion of an asset’s variability that is uncorrelated with the market is eliminated within the market portfolio and therefore commands no expected return. This is precisely why the Capital Asset Pricing Model prices beta rather than total volatility: in a world where everyone holds the market, only systematic risk remains to be compensated.

Qualifications

The theory is exact; its inputs are not. The frontier depends on expected returns, standard deviations, and correlations that must be estimated from historical data, and these estimates are imprecise. Small changes in estimated expected returns can shift the computed tangency portfolio substantially, so portfolios optimised mechanically on historical inputs are often concentrated and unstable. A second qualification is that correlations are not constant: in severe market declines many assets fall together, so correlations rise and the diversification benefit is smallest at the moment it is most wanted. A third is that real investors frequently hold too little international exposure, a pattern termed home bias, which leaves attainable diversification unused. None of these qualifications overturns the central conclusion. They argue for broad, low-cost, value-weighted holdings rebalanced infrequently — which is to say, for approximating the market portfolio rather than attempting to out-optimise it.

Exercise 10.1 Easy

Portfolio A has an expected return of 9 percent and a standard deviation of 14 percent. The risk-free rate is 2 percent. Compute A’s Sharpe ratio.

Solution

S = (9% − 2%) / 14% = 7/14 = 0.50. The portfolio earns half a percentage point of excess return for each percentage point of standard deviation. A higher ratio is preferred; the tangency portfolio is the one that maximises it.

Exercise 10.2 Easy

Two assets each have a standard deviation of 20 percent. Asset X has an expected return of 10 percent and asset Y of 6 percent. If their correlation is 0.3, what is the standard deviation of an equally weighted portfolio, and how does it compare with 20 percent?

Solution

σ_p² = 0.5²(0.20²) + 0.5²(0.20²) + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.3)(0.20)(0.20) = 0.01 + 0.006 = 0.016, so σ_p = 12.6%.

The portfolio standard deviation of 12.6 percent is well below the 20 percent common to both assets, even though the expected return is the average of the two. Combining assets that are not perfectly correlated lowers risk without lowering expected return.

Exercise 10.3 Medium

An investor wants less risk than the tangency portfolio provides. According to the separation theorem, what should she do, and what should she avoid doing?

Solution

She should continue to hold the tangency (market) portfolio and place part of her wealth in the risk-free asset. This moves her down the capital allocation line to lower risk while preserving the highest attainable Sharpe ratio.

She should avoid switching to a different, apparently safer, risky portfolio made of conservative securities. Any risky portfolio other than the tangency portfolio lies below the capital allocation line and offers a lower expected return for the risk taken. Risk preference is expressed through the division between the market portfolio and the risk-free asset, not through a change in the risky holdings.

Exercise 10.4 Medium

Using the tangency portfolio from the worked example (expected return 8.1 percent, standard deviation 12.8 percent, risk-free rate 3 percent), find the expected return and standard deviation of a position that borrows an amount equal to 50 percent of wealth at the risk-free rate and invests 150 percent in the tangency portfolio.

Solution

E[r_p] = 1.5(8.1%) − 0.5(3%) = 12.15% − 1.5% = 10.65%. σ_p = 1.5(12.8%) = 19.2%.

The Sharpe ratio is (10.65% − 3%) / 19.2% = 0.40, the same as the unlevered tangency portfolio. Borrowing to invest moves the investor up the same capital allocation line: higher expected return and higher risk, with the reward-to-risk ratio unchanged.

Exercise 10.5 Hard

An acquaintance reports that his portfolio returned 22 percent last year while a broad market index returned 15 percent. Identify two questions that should be answered before concluding that his approach is superior.

Solution

First, how much risk was taken? A higher raw return obtained by accepting substantially higher risk is not clearly better. The appropriate comparison is the Sharpe ratio. If his portfolio’s standard deviation was roughly double the market’s, his reward per unit of risk may be lower than the index’s despite the higher return.

Second, is the result attributable to skill or to chance? A single year is a very small sample. Among a large population of investors, some will exceed the market in any given year through chance alone (Chapter 11). Only sustained risk-adjusted outperformance over many years constitutes evidence of skill, and such evidence is rare. A one-year raw return, considered without reference to risk or sample size, carries little information.

Exercise 10.6 Hard

Explain, with reference to the two-asset variance formula, why the minimum-variance combination of Northwind shares and the bond fund still holds about 11 percent in shares rather than zero, even though the bond fund is far less volatile on its own.

Solution

Starting from a position held entirely in bonds, adding a small amount of Northwind shares changes portfolio variance through two offsetting effects. The own-variance terms rise, because shares are more volatile; but the covariance term, with a correlation of only 0.10, adds little joint variability and partly substitutes for bond variability that is removed as the bond weight falls.

At a zero share weight the second effect dominates at the margin, so the first units of shares reduce total variance rather than increase it. The variance stops falling, and the minimum is reached, only once the share weight has risen to about 11 percent. This is why the least-risky portfolio contains some of the riskier asset: the benefit of weak correlation outweighs the cost of higher own-variance until that point.

Market Efficiency & Behavior

📖 28 min read
📈 2 figures
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A central question for any investor is whether security prices can be forecast well enough to earn returns beyond those warranted by risk. The efficient markets hypothesis holds that they cannot, because prices already incorporate the information available to market participants. This chapter states the hypothesis in its three conventional forms, reviews the empirical evidence — most notably the long-run performance of active managers relative to index benchmarks — and then examines the behavioral regularities that cause prices to depart from the efficient benchmark, together with the reasons those departures are difficult to exploit.

In 2007 Warren Buffett entered a publicly recorded ten-year wager that a low-cost fund tracking the S&P 500 index would outperform a selected group of funds of hedge funds, measured after fees. The actively managed alternatives employed large research staffs and charged substantial fees for their analysis. When the period closed in 2017, the index fund had returned considerably more than the active managers. The outcome is consistent with the central prediction developed in this chapter: after costs, active management does not, on average, outperform a broad market index.

Information and prices

The efficient markets hypothesis (EMH) states that asset prices fully reflect the information available to market participants. The reasoning is competitive. If a security were known to be underpriced relative to its fundamentals, profit-seeking investors would buy it, and their buying would raise the price until the discrepancy disappeared. The act of exploiting a mispricing is also the act of removing it. By the time a piece of information is widely available, prices have already adjusted to it, and the information is said to be reflected in the price.

A direct consequence is that prices change only in response to new information, which by definition cannot be predicted from what is already known. Successive price changes are therefore approximately unpredictable, a property described as a random walk: the best forecast of tomorrow’s price, given everything known today, is today’s price adjusted for the normal expected return. This does not mean prices are random in the sense of arbitrary; it means the unexpected part of each price change is unforecastable. An abnormal return — the return on a security in excess of the return warranted by its risk — should not be earnable on a systematic basis from information already in the public domain.

Three forms of efficiency

The hypothesis is conventionally stated at three levels, distinguished by the information set that prices are said to reflect. Weak-form efficiency holds that prices reflect all information contained in the history of past prices and trading volumes; it implies that technical analysis — the study of past price patterns to forecast future prices — cannot produce systematic abnormal returns. Semi-strong-form efficiency holds that prices reflect all publicly available information, including financial statements, announcements, and news; it implies that fundamental analysis of public information — the valuation methods of Chapters 6 and 7 — cannot by itself produce a reliable edge, because others have already acted on the same information. Strong-form efficiency holds that prices reflect even private information; this form is not generally true, and trading on material non-public information is in any case illegal. The semi-strong form carries the practical lesson: information that is public is already priced, so publicly known recommendations confer no systematic advantage.

Evidence from an earnings announcement

The semi-strong form makes a testable prediction about how prices respond to news, which can be examined through an event study — a measurement of a security’s abnormal return in the days surrounding a specific event. Consider a quarterly earnings announcement by Northwind Components. Analysts’ consensus expectation — the average of their forecasts — is earnings of $1.75 per share. Northwind reports $1.90. The earnings surprise is the difference relative to expectations, (1.90 − 1.75) / 1.75 = 8.6%. Only this surprise component is news; the expected $1.75 was already reflected in the price.

The figure plots the cumulative abnormal return (CAR) — the running total of Northwind’s abnormal returns — across the trading days around the announcement. The efficient pattern is a single adjustment on the announcement day, with no systematic movement before (the surprise was not known) or after (the information has been fully incorporated). A contrasting pattern, in which the price drifts toward its new level only gradually over subsequent days, would indicate that the market was slow to incorporate the information, and would offer a forecastable return to a trader acting after the announcement.

The efficient pattern is close to what is observed for large, widely followed securities, where prices adjust within seconds. For smaller and less-followed securities a modest post-announcement drift has been documented — an example of an anomaly, a persistent and statistically reliable departure from the efficient benchmark. Anomalies are of considerable research interest, but most are small relative to trading costs and tend to weaken after they are published and widely known, which is itself consistent with the efficiency mechanism.

The record of active management

The EMH implies that professional managers who actively select securities should not, on average, outperform a broad market index after costs. The evidence supports this prediction consistently. Across markets and horizons, the majority of actively managed funds underperform their benchmark index after fees, and the proportion that underperforms rises as the horizon lengthens. The small number of funds that outperform in one period rarely repeat, as would be expected if their results reflected chance rather than durable skill.

The result rests on an identity as much as on a hypothesis, an argument set out by William Sharpe as the arithmetic of active management. All investors together hold the entire market, so collectively they earn the market return before costs. Investors who hold the market index capture that return at very low cost. The remaining investors — those who trade actively — also collectively hold the market and therefore also earn the market return before costs, but they incur much higher fees and trading expenses. After costs, active investors as a group must therefore earn less than the index, by exactly the additional costs they bear.

Worked example
The arithmetic, with figures

Suppose the market returns 8.0 percent in a year. An index fund charging 0.04 percent returns 7.96 percent. Active investors as a group also earn 8.0 percent before costs, but at an average cost of 0.90 percent they return 7.10 percent — trailing the index investor by about 0.86 percentage points.

A single year’s gap is small, but it compounds. Over thirty years, $10,000 compounding at 7.96 percent grows to about $99,500, while the same sum at 7.10 percent grows to about $78,300 — a difference of roughly $21,000 produced entirely by the cost differential, not by any difference in skill. Fees, rather than security selection, are the decisive variable over long horizons.

Two measurement cautions reinforce the conclusion rather than weaken it. Survivorship bias — the tendency for poorly performing funds to be closed and dropped from databases — flatters the measured record of active funds, so the true average is worse than it appears. And comparisons must be made against a benchmark of matched risk; a fund that beats a broad index by taking greater risk has not demonstrated skill, only leverage to the market.

Behavioral departures from efficiency

Markets are composed of people, and people depart from the fully rational benchmark in regular, documented ways. Behavioral finance studies these departures and their effect on prices. Several patterns recur. Overconfidence leads investors to overestimate the precision of their judgments, to trade more frequently than is warranted, and to incur costs that reduce their returns. Herding is the tendency to follow the actions of others, buying because others are buying; it can push prices far from fundamentals and contributes to the formation of asset-price bubbles. Loss aversion — the finding that losses are felt more acutely than equivalent gains — produces the disposition effect, the tendency to sell securities that have risen and to retain those that have fallen, in order to avoid realising a loss.

Anchoring is the reliance on an arbitrary reference point, such as the price originally paid, when forming a judgment of value. Extrapolation, or recency bias, is the tendency to project the recent past indefinitely into the future, which inflates prices during sustained rises and depresses them during declines. Mental accounting is the practice of treating money differently according to its source or label — spending a windfall freely while guarding ordinary income — in a way that departs from treating wealth as fungible. These regularities help explain episodes that are difficult to reconcile with full efficiency, such as the technology-stock boom that ended in 2000 and the housing-credit expansion that preceded the 2008 financial crisis.

Why mispricings persist: limits to arbitrage

If behavioral forces push prices away from fundamentals, one might expect rational investors to profit by trading against the mispricing and, in doing so, to correct it. In practice this correction is constrained, and the constraints are collectively termed the limits to arbitrage. A mispricing can widen before it narrows: an overvalued security can become more overvalued, and an investor who has sold it short may face losses large enough to force the position to be closed before the correction arrives. Being correct about value but early in timing can be indistinguishable, in its financial consequences, from being wrong. This is noise-trader risk — the risk that the irrational behavior driving a mispricing intensifies rather than reverses.

A related point concerns the incentives to gather information. If prices already reflected all information perfectly, no investor would be compensated for the costly work of analysis, and so none would undertake it; but if no one analysed, prices would cease to reflect information. Markets must therefore be slightly inefficient — sufficiently so to reward the analysts whose activity keeps prices informative. This observation, associated with Grossman and Stiglitz, implies that perfect efficiency is self-contradicting, and that a small, hard-won degree of inefficiency is a permanent feature of informationally active markets.

A measured synthesis

Markets are efficient enough that an ordinary investor should not expect to outperform them by trading on public information, yet not so perfectly efficient that prices never reach extremes. Both propositions are supported by the evidence. The disciplined response is not to search for the market’s errors but to control one’s own: a diversified portfolio, held at low cost over a long horizon, captures the market return, while most active trading transfers wealth to costs and to the consequences of behavioral error.

Exercise 11.5 Hard

If behavioral biases create genuine mispricings, why does their existence not make outperformance straightforward? Give two reasons.

Solution

First, timing is uncertain and costly. A mispricing can intensify before it corrects, and a position taken against it can incur losses large enough to be closed before the correction arrives. Being correct about value but early in timing produces the same losses as being wrong — the limits-to-arbitrage argument, of which noise-trader risk is the central element.

Second, the biases operate on the investor as well. The same tendencies that create mispricings — herding, extrapolation, loss aversion — act on anyone attempting to exploit them. Recognising a bubble intellectually does not prevent one from following the crowd into it or abandoning a position at the point of maximum pessimism. By the time a mispricing is unambiguous, informed traders have usually removed it. Behavioral finance therefore explains market history more successfully than it supplies a reliable trading advantage; its most dependable application is the disciplined management of one’s own conduct.

Exercise 11.6 Hard

Explain the Grossman–Stiglitz observation and why it implies that markets cannot be perfectly efficient.

Solution

Gathering and analysing information is costly. Investors will undertake it only if they are compensated — that is, only if their analysis earns an abnormal return. But if prices already reflected all information perfectly, analysis would earn nothing, and no one would perform it.

If no one analysed, however, prices would no longer reflect information. The two conditions cannot hold simultaneously. Markets must therefore be slightly inefficient, by an amount just sufficient to reward the analysis that keeps prices informative. Perfect informational efficiency is self-contradicting; a small, persistent degree of inefficiency is a structural feature of active markets. The practical margin is nonetheless too small, after costs, for the typical investor to capture.

Options & Derivatives

📖 34 min read
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A derivative is a contract whose value is determined by something else — a stock, a currency, a commodity, an interest rate. The most important derivatives are options, which confer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price. That asymmetry gives options remarkable flexibility: a single contract can insure a portfolio, finance a cautious income strategy, or express a precise view on the direction or the volatility of a price. This chapter develops the instruments formally, works each strategy through with numbers, and reads the payoff diagram that makes the economics visible. Throughout, the underlying is a share of Northwind Components, trading at $28.

A wheat farmer fears the harvest price will fall; an airline fears a rise in the price of jet fuel; an investor approaching retirement fears a market decline in the year before she stops working. Each confronts a risk that someone else would willingly assume for a fee. Derivatives are the contracts that move risk from the party that wishes to shed it to the party prepared to carry it. The same contract that allows the farmer to lock in a price can, in other hands, serve as a highly leveraged wager. The purpose of this chapter is to make that distinction precise, and the payoff diagram is the instrument that reveals it.

Forwards and futures: the simplest derivatives

Before options, consider the most basic derivative: an agreement today to transact at a future date at a price fixed now. A forward contract is a private agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set date; a futures contract is the same idea standardised and traded on an exchange, with daily settlement of gains and losses. Unlike an option, a forward carries an obligation: both parties must transact, whatever the market price turns out to be.

A numerical illustration. Suppose Northwind must purchase 100,000 pounds of copper in six months and fears a price increase. Copper trades today at $4.00 per pound, and a six-month futures contract is available at $4.10. By buying futures at $4.10, Northwind fixes its cost. If copper rises to $4.60, the futures position gains $0.50 per pound ($50,000), offsetting the higher purchase price; if copper falls to $3.70, the futures position loses $0.40 per pound, which exactly cancels the saving on the cheaper purchase. Either way the effective cost is locked at $4.10. The hedge removes both the risk of loss and the possibility of gain: that symmetry is the defining feature of a forward, and the first reason an option — which removes only the downside — commands a premium.

Calls and puts

Two instruments generate the entire options universe. A call option grants its holder the right to buy the underlying at a fixed strike price on or before expiration. An investor buys a call in anticipation of a price increase: if the share rises above the strike, the holder exercises and captures the difference; if it does not, the holder allows the option to expire and forfeits only the premium paid. A put option is the mirror image — the right to sell at the strike — and it gains value as the price falls, which makes it a form of insurance on an owned asset.

Definition
Call & put options

A call is the right, not the obligation, to buy at the strike price; its value rises with the underlying. A put is the right to sell at the strike; its value rises as the underlying falls. The buyer pays a premium at the outset. The buyer’s maximum loss is that premium, while the potential gain is large. The seller (writer) receives the premium and assumes the corresponding obligation, with the risk profile reversed.

Two further terms recur throughout. To be long an instrument is to own it — to have bought it and to gain when its value rises; to be short is to have sold it, taking on the opposite exposure. The buyer of a call thus holds a long call and the buyer of a put a long put, while the party who sells an option, collecting the premium and assuming the obligation, is said to write it, or to be short the option.

An option is described by its moneyness. A call is in the money when the share price exceeds the strike, at the money when the two coincide, and out of the money when the share trades below the strike; for a put the conditions are reversed. With Northwind at $28, a call struck at $25 is in the money, a call struck at $28 is at the money, and a call struck at $32 is out of the money.

Intrinsic value and time value

The premium of an option divides into two components. Intrinsic value is the amount by which the option is in the money — what it would be worth if it expired immediately. For a call it equals the greater of zero and the share price minus the strike. Time value is the remainder of the premium: the amount a buyer pays for the possibility that the option moves further into the money before expiration.

Consider a Northwind call struck at $26 with three months to expiration, trading at a premium of $3.00 while the share is at $28. Its intrinsic value is $\max(28-26,0)=\$2.00$; the remaining $\$1.00$ is time value. As expiration approaches, time value decays toward zero, so that at expiry an option is worth exactly its intrinsic value. This decay, called theta, is examined below among the option’s sensitivities.

Reading the payoff diagram

Every option position is clarified by its payoff diagram, which plots profit or loss per share (vertical axis) against the price of the underlying at expiration (horizontal axis). It is important to distinguish the payoff, which is the option’s value at expiry, from the profit, which subtracts the premium paid. The diagrams in this chapter plot profit, so the breakeven point — where profit crosses zero — is shown explicitly.

A long call struck at $30 for a premium of $1.50 is flat at a loss of $1.50 up to the strike, then rises one-for-one with the share. The maximum loss is the premium; the breakeven is the strike plus the premium, $31.50; the upside is, in principle, unlimited. At a share price of $34 the option is worth $4.00 and the profit is $2.50.

A long put struck at $26 for a premium of $1.20 is the reflection: it profits as the share falls, with a maximum loss equal to the premium and a breakeven at the strike minus the premium, $24.80. At a share price of $22 the put is worth $4.00 and the profit is $2.80.

A toolkit of strategies

Because calls, puts, and the share itself can be combined, an investor can construct a position for almost any view. The strategies below are the standard building blocks, each worked through on Northwind shares and each accompanied by its payoff diagram.

The covered call: trading upside for income

An investor who owns 100 Northwind shares at $28 and expects little movement may write a call against them, struck at $31, collecting a premium of $1.00. If the share stays below $31, the call expires and the premium is kept. If the share rises above $31, the shares are called away at $31, and the total gain is the $3.00 appreciation plus the $1.00 premium, capped at $4.00. The premium also cushions a decline, lowering the breakeven to $27.00. The cost of the strategy is the surrender of any gain beyond $31.

The protective put: insuring a holding

The same shareholder, instead fearing a decline, may buy a put struck at $26 for $1.20. This establishes a floor: below $26 the loss on the shares is offset by the gain on the put, so the worst outcome is a loss of $3.20 per share (the $2.00 decline to the strike plus the $1.20 premium). The upside is retained, reduced only by the premium, with a breakeven of $29.20. A protective put is insurance in the strict sense: a premium paid to cap the loss while preserving the gain.

The bull call spread: a bounded bullish bet

An investor who expects a moderate rise, and wishes to reduce the premium, may buy a call struck at $28 for $2.00 and simultaneously sell a call struck at $32 for $0.80, a net outlay of $1.20. The sold call caps the gain but finances much of the purchase. The maximum profit is $2.80 (the $4.00 distance between strikes minus the $1.20 net premium), reached at or above $32; the maximum loss is the $1.20 net premium, incurred at or below $28; the breakeven is $29.20. The spread trades away unlimited upside for a cheaper, defined-risk position.

The long straddle: a position on volatility

An investor who expects a large move but is uncertain of its direction — before an earnings announcement, say — may buy both a call and a put at the $28 strike, for premiums of $2.00 and $1.80, a total of $3.80. A large move in either direction carries one option deep into the money; a small move leaves both nearly worthless. The breakevens lie $3.80 on each side of the strike, at $24.20 and $31.80, and the maximum loss, $3.80, occurs if the share closes exactly at $28. A straddle is a position on volatility itself rather than on direction.

Two further combinations complete the standard set. A bear put spread is the mirror of the bull call spread: buy a put struck at $28 for $1.80 and sell a put struck at $24 for $0.60, a net cost of $1.20, profiting from a moderate decline with a maximum gain of $2.80 and a maximum loss of $1.20. A collar combines the two protective strategies on an owned share: buy a $26 put for $1.20 and sell a $31 call for $1.00, a net cost of $0.20, which fixes both a floor and a ceiling and is a common, low-cost way to protect an accumulated gain. What determines an option’s price

An option’s premium responds to six variables, and the direction of each effect follows from the logic of a right without an obligation. A higher share price raises a call and lowers a put. A higher strike does the reverse. More time to expiration raises both, since it widens the range of outcomes that may occur. Greater volatility raises both as well — this is the central and least intuitive point: because the holder is protected against adverse moves, a wider distribution of outcomes is pure benefit, so a more volatile underlying carries a more valuable option. A higher risk-free rate raises a call and lowers a put, and larger dividends do the reverse.

Pricing by replication: the one-period binomial model

How is the premium itself determined? The foundational insight is that an option can be replicated by a position in the share and a risk-free loan, and that two portfolios with identical payoffs must have identical prices, or arbitrage would follow. Consider one period over which a Northwind share, now $28, either rises to $33.60 or falls to $23.80, with a risk-free rate of 5 percent for the period. Value a call struck at $28. In the up state the call is worth $\max(33.60-28,0)=\$5.60$; in the down state it is worth $0.

Form a portfolio of $\Delta$ shares and a loan. Choose $\Delta$ so the portfolio matches the call in both states: $\Delta = (5.60-0)/(33.60-23.80) = 5.60/9.80 = 0.571$ shares. This hedge ratio is the option’s delta. Holding 0.571 shares and borrowing the present value of the lower payoff, $12.95, reproduces the call exactly, so the call must cost $0.571 \times 28 - 12.95 = \$3.05$. Equivalently, define the risk-neutral probability $q=(1.05-0.85)/(1.20-0.85)=0.571$ and discount the expected payoff: $(0.571 \times 5.60)/1.05 = \$3.05$. Both routes give the same premium, because both rest on the same no-arbitrage principle. Extending this lattice to many small steps yields the celebrated Black–Scholes formula, which the Options Lab uses to price every contract.

Put–call parity

Calls and puts on the same share are bound together by a no-arbitrage identity, put–call parity: a call minus a put, same strike and expiry, equals the share price minus the present value of the strike. For Northwind, with the call at $3.05, a share at $28, a strike of $28, and a 5 percent rate, the put must be worth $3.05 - 28 + 28/1.05 = \$1.72$. Valuing the put directly in the binomial lattice gives the same figure, confirming the relationship. The economic content is that owning a share and a protective put produces the same payoff as owning a call and holding cash; if the two ever diverged in price, an arbitrageur would trade the difference away. This impossibility of free money is the foundation of all derivative pricing.

The Greeks: how an option responds

Practitioners summarise an option’s sensitivities in a set of measures known as the Greeks. Delta is the change in the option’s value for a one-dollar change in the share; the Northwind call above has a delta of about 0.57, so it gains roughly 57 cents when the share rises a dollar, and delta also serves as the hedge ratio. Gamma measures how delta itself changes as the share moves, and is largest for at-the-money options near expiry. Theta is the loss of value as time passes — the decay of time value — and works against the buyer. Vega measures sensitivity to volatility, and is positive for both calls and puts, which is the quantitative form of the point that volatility raises option values. A trader who hedges delta and gamma can hold a position whose value is largely insulated from small moves in the share, isolating a view on volatility or time.

Hedging, speculation, and leverage

The same option may serve as insurance or as a wager, according to the holder’s position and purpose. A shareholder who buys puts against a portfolio is hedging: paying a premium to cap a loss, precisely as one insures a house. A trader who commits savings to far-out-of-the-money calls is speculating: using the option’s leverage — a small premium controls a large exposure — to make an amplified, all-or-nothing bet. Options are neither prudent nor reckless in themselves; they are instruments that transfer and reshape risk. The leverage that makes them efficient for hedging is the same leverage that makes them hazardous for the speculator.

Why derivatives can be dangerous at scale

Leverage operates in both directions. Because a small premium commands a large exposure, derivatives allow institutions to assume enormous risks with little visible capital, which is advantageous until the exposure moves against them. A substantial part of the 2008 financial crisis can be traced to derivatives — contracts linked to mortgages — whose risks were poorly measured and vast in scale. The instruments that allow a farmer to hedge a harvest can, when misused at scale, threaten the financial system. The leverage warrants respect.

The sold call forfeits all gain beyond $32 in exchange for reducing the cost from $2.00 to $1.20 — a defined-risk position for a moderate view.

Exercise 12.4 Medium

You own Northwind at $28 and buy a protective put struck at $26 for $1.20. What is the worst loss you can suffer per share, and at what price is your position back to breakeven?

Solution

Below $26 the put gains dollar-for-dollar as the share falls, so losses stop. The worst loss is the $2.00 decline from $28 to the $26 strike plus the $1.20 premium: $3.20 per share. Breakeven is 28 + 1.20 = $29.20, the purchase price plus the insurance cost.

The upside is undiminished except for the premium — the exact profile of an insurance policy with a deductible.

Exercise 12.5 Hard

Over one period a Northwind share, now $28, either rises to $33.60 or falls to $23.80; the risk-free rate is 5% for the period. Using replication, find the price of a call struck at $28 and state its delta.

Solution

Call payoffs: up max(33.60 − 28, 0) = 5.60; down 0. Delta = (5.60 − 0)/(33.60 − 23.80) = 5.60/9.80 = 0.571 shares.

Holding 0.571 shares and borrowing the present value of the down-state value, (0.571 × 23.80)/1.05 = $12.95, replicates the call, so the call is worth 0.571 × 28 − 12.95 = $3.05. The risk-neutral route agrees: q = (1.05 − 0.85)/(1.20 − 0.85) = 0.571, and (0.571 × 5.60)/1.05 = $3.05.

Exercise 12.6 Hard

“Options are merely gambling.” Explain in what sense this is sometimes accurate and why it misrepresents the economic purpose of options.

Solution

Sometimes accurate: a trader who commits money he cannot afford to lose to far-out-of-the-money calls is using leverage to make an all-or-nothing wager, most of which expire worthless. Employed in this way, an option functions as a lottery ticket.

But the purpose is the opposite: options exist to transfer and reshape risk, not to manufacture it. A farmer fixing a harvest price, an airline capping fuel costs, and an investor insuring a portfolio with puts are all reducing risk, paying a premium to transfer danger to a party better placed to bear it. The economic function of derivatives is to allow risk to flow to whoever values it least; the gambling use is a misapplication of an instrument whose core role is insurance.

Budgeting, Credit & Debt

📖 30 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
📈 2 figures
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

The analytical tools developed for firms — cash-flow accounting, compounding, discounting, and the balance sheet — apply with equal force to the finances of an individual or household. This chapter applies them to three foundational tasks: constructing a budget, understanding the mechanics and cost of consumer credit, and retiring debt efficiently. Together these determine the saving rate on which the wealth-building of Chapter 14 depends.

Consider two individuals with identical incomes of $60,000. One concludes each year having saved a portion of income and carrying no high-interest debt; the other carries a revolving credit-card balance accruing interest at 22 percent. Over a decade the two financial positions diverge substantially — one accumulating invested savings, the other transferring a growing share of income to lenders as interest. The divergence originates not in income, which is equal, but in the difference between income and expenditure, and in how that difference is directed.

The household cash-flow statement

A budget is a personal cash-flow statement: income received, expenditure made, and the difference between them. That difference, when positive, is saving — the surplus of income over expenditure — and it is the input to the compounding process of Chapter 3. Expenditure is usefully divided into fixed expenses, which recur at a set amount (rent or a mortgage payment, insurance, loan payments), and variable expenses, which fluctuate with choices and circumstances (food, transport, discretionary purchases). A budget is not a constraint imposed from outside; it is a measurement that makes the saving rate visible and therefore manageable.

The most effective procedural habit is to reverse the usual order of operations. Rather than spending first and saving whatever remains, the household should direct a fixed share of income to saving immediately on receipt — a practice termed paying yourself first — and then meet expenditure from the remainder, ideally by an automatic transfer scheduled for payday. A common allocation framework is the 50/30/20 rule: approximately 50 percent of after-tax income to needs, 30 percent to wants, and 20 percent to saving and debt repayment. A more exacting method is zero-based budgeting, in which every unit of income is assigned a specific purpose — expenditure, saving, or debt repayment — until the unallocated amount is zero. The precise framework matters less than the presence of one and the consistency of its application.

The central relationship

Wealth accumulates from the difference between income and expenditure, invested and left to compound. That difference can be widened from either side, by raising income or reducing expenditure, but it is the difference itself, not the level of income, that compounds. A high income with no surplus accumulates nothing; a moderate income with a steady surplus, invested over several decades, accumulates substantial wealth (Chapter 3). The remainder of personal finance is detail built upon this relationship.

The household balance sheet and net worth

Alongside the cash-flow statement sits a personal balance sheet, constructed exactly as a firm’s balance sheet in Chapter 6. Assets are what the household owns — cash and deposits, investment accounts, a home, a vehicle. Liabilities are what it owes — a mortgage, student loans, vehicle financing, credit-card balances. Net worth is the difference, net worth = assets − liabilities. Where the budget measures the flow of money over a period, the balance sheet measures the stock of wealth at a point in time; a positive saving rate sustained over time is what causes net worth to rise.

Worked example
A household net-worth statement

A household holds $8,000 in cash, $42,000 in an investment account, a home valued at $280,000, and a vehicle worth $15,000 — total assets of $345,000. Against these it owes a mortgage of $215,000, a student loan of $18,000, and a credit-card balance of $4,000 — total liabilities of $237,000.

Net worth is $345,000 − $237,000 = $108,000. Tracking this single figure over time is the clearest measure of financial progress: it rises when the household saves and when its assets appreciate, and it falls when debts grow faster than assets. Note that a large asset (the home) is accompanied by a large liability (the mortgage); net worth, not the headline value of any asset, is the quantity that matters.

A component of the asset side deserves separate treatment: the emergency fund, a reserve of readily accessible cash sufficient to cover three to six months of essential expenses. Its purpose is liquidity — the capacity to meet an unexpected expense or a loss of income without resorting to high-interest borrowing. An emergency fund earns a low return and will, in isolation, appear to be an inefficient holding; its value lies in preventing the far costlier outcome of financing an emergency on a credit card at 22 percent.

How consumer credit works

A credit card is a form of revolving credit: a borrowing limit against which the holder may draw repeatedly, with no fixed repayment schedule beyond a required minimum. Its cost is expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR), the stated annual interest rate; the interest actually charged accrues at the monthly periodic rate, which is the APR divided by twelve. A balance of $6,000 at a 22 percent APR accrues interest in the first month of $6,000 × (0.22 / 12) = $110. Because interest compounds on any unpaid balance, the effective annual cost is somewhat higher than the stated APR, as Chapter 2 showed for compounding within the year.

Used as a means of payment and settled in full each month within the grace period — the interval between a purchase and the payment due date, during which no interest accrues on purchases — a credit card carries no interest cost and may return value through rewards and fraud protection. Carried as a balance, it is among the most expensive forms of borrowing available to a household. The structural reason is the minimum payment, which is typically set only slightly above the monthly interest. A payment barely exceeding the interest charge reduces the principal very slowly, so the balance persists for years and the cumulative interest can approach or exceed the amount originally borrowed. Amortizing debt

Most other household borrowing — a mortgage, a vehicle loan, a student loan — takes the form of an amortizing loan: a debt repaid through a fixed, regular payment over a set term, calculated so that the balance reaches zero at the end. The payment is determined by the loan amount, the periodic interest rate, and the number of payments, using the annuity relation of Chapter 4. Each payment is divided between interest, charged on the outstanding balance, and principal, which reduces that balance. Because the balance is largest at the outset, the interest portion is largest at the outset and the principal portion smallest; as the balance declines, the split shifts progressively toward principal. This process is called amortization, and it is the opposite of revolving credit, which has no fixed schedule and no defined end date.

The figure shows the composition of the fixed monthly payment on a 30-year, $300,000 mortgage at a 6 percent APR, for which the payment is about $1,799. In the first month roughly $1,500 of that payment is interest and only about $300 reduces the principal; the two portions become equal only after approximately nineteen years, and only thereafter does the majority of each payment reduce the balance. Over the full term the total interest paid is about $347,500 — more than the amount originally borrowed. The front-loading of interest explains why early additional payments, applied directly to principal, are especially effective: a dollar of principal removed early avoids interest in every remaining month.

Credit scores

A credit score is a numerical summary of a borrower’s credit history, used by lenders to estimate the likelihood of repayment and to set the interest rate offered. It is computed from the information in a credit report, the record of a borrower’s accounts and payment history maintained by credit bureaus. A higher score lowers the cost of future borrowing, so the score has direct financial value: on a mortgage, the difference between a high and a moderate score can amount to a materially different interest rate and many thousands of dollars of interest over the term.

The largest determinants of the score are payment history — whether obligations have been met on time — and the credit utilization ratio, the fraction of available revolving credit currently in use. A utilization ratio held well below the available limit is treated as a sign of lower risk. The length of credit history, the mix of credit types, and the frequency of applications for new credit contribute further. The practical implications are straightforward: pay obligations on time without exception, keep revolving balances low relative to limits, and apply for new credit only when needed.

Classifying debt and ordering repayment

Debt is not uniform. Borrowing that finances an asset expected to generate value or income — a mortgage on a residence, a student loan for education that raises earnings, a loan for a productive business — is generally undertaken at a moderate rate and may be economically justified. Borrowing that finances consumption at a high rate — a revolving credit-card balance, a payday loan, financing of a depreciating vehicle beyond the borrower’s means — is not. The relevant criterion is the interest rate relative to what the borrowed funds produce: borrowing at 22 percent to acquire items that generate no return reduces wealth over time.

A useful summary measure is the debt-to-income ratio, the share of gross income committed to debt payments, which lenders use to judge borrowing capacity and which a household can use to monitor its own position. When several debts are held simultaneously, two repayment orderings are commonly compared, each applied after the minimums on all debts have been met. The avalanche method directs every additional unit of repayment to the debt with the highest interest rate; it minimises total interest paid. The snowball method directs additional repayment to the smallest balance first; it is marginally more costly in interest but produces earlier completed payoffs, which can sustain adherence to the plan.

Worked example
Avalanche versus snowball

A household holds three debts — $2,000 at 24 percent, $1,000 at 15 percent, and $5,000 at 10 percent — and can direct a total of $400 per month to repayment. The avalanche method attacks the 24 percent debt first, then the 15 percent, then the 10 percent. The snowball method attacks the $1,000 balance first, then the $2,000, then the $5,000.

Both plans clear all three debts in about 23 months. The avalanche pays approximately $958 in total interest; the snowball pays approximately $1,019 — about $61 more. The avalanche is the lower-cost ordering, as it must be, but the difference here is modest. Where the behavioral benefit of an early completed payoff materially improves the likelihood of completing the plan, the snowball can be the better practical choice. The ordering that is actually sustained is preferable to the theoretically optimal ordering that is abandoned.

One implication connects this chapter to the investment chapters. Repaying a debt is economically equivalent to earning a return equal to the debt’s interest rate, with certainty and without tax. Repaying a balance that charges 22 percent therefore produces a guaranteed 22 percent return — higher than the expected return on any ordinary investment, and without risk. A household that holds low-yield savings while carrying high-rate debt is, in effect, lending at a low rate and borrowing at a high one; aside from a necessary emergency reserve, directing those funds to the debt is almost always the superior use.

After-tax income is $4,000 per month. Under the 50/30/20 rule, how much is allocated to needs, to wants, and to saving and debt repayment?

Solution

Exercise 13.2 Easy

A household owns assets of $60,000 and owes $44,000. State its net worth, and explain what a rising net worth over time indicates.

Solution

Net worth is $60,000 − $44,000 = $16,000. A net worth that rises over time indicates that the household is saving, that its assets are appreciating, or that it is reducing its debts — in combination, that wealth is accumulating. It is the single most informative summary of financial progress, because it nets what is owned against what is owed.

Exercise 13.3 Medium

Using the payoff calculator for a $6,000 balance at a 22 percent APR, compare a payment of $150 per month with one of $300 per month. Approximately how much time and interest does the larger payment save?

Solution

At $150 per month the balance takes about six years to clear and accrues several thousand dollars in interest; at $300 per month it clears in roughly two years with substantially less interest — the calculator reports the exact figures. Doubling the payment reduces both the term and the interest by more than half, because far less time is spent paying interest on a persisting balance.

The general point: on high-interest debt, each additional unit of payment earns a guaranteed return equal to the APR. Directing funds to a 22 percent balance is equivalent to a risk-free 22 percent return, which exceeds what is available from ordinary investments.

Exercise 13.4 Medium

On the 30-year, $300,000 mortgage at 6 percent described in the text, the first monthly payment is about $1,799, of which roughly $1,500 is interest. Compute the interest in the first month directly, and explain why the principal portion grows in later months.

Solution

The monthly periodic rate is 6% / 12 = 0.5%. First-month interest is $300,000 × 0.005 = $1,500, so the principal portion is $1,799 − $1,500 = $299.

In the next month interest is charged on the slightly smaller balance of $300,000 − $299 = $299,701, so the interest portion is marginally lower and the principal portion marginally higher. Because the payment is fixed, every reduction in the balance shifts a little more of the next payment from interest to principal, and the shift accelerates as the balance falls.

Exercise 13.5 Hard

A household holds $3,000 in a savings account earning 4 percent and a $3,000 credit-card balance charging 22 percent. What should it do, and what single qualification should accompany the decision?

Solution

The household should use most of the savings to repay the credit-card balance. Holding the two positions simultaneously earns 4 percent while paying 22 percent — a net loss of about 18 percent per year on $3,000, roughly $540 annually. Repaying the 22 percent balance is equivalent to a guaranteed, risk-free return of 22 percent, which exceeds any ordinary investment return.

The qualification is that a modest emergency reserve should be retained, so that the next unexpected expense does not have to be financed again at 22 percent. The household must also avoid rebuilding the balance. Subject to that reserve, holding low-yield savings against high-rate debt is among the most common and costly inconsistencies in household finance.

Exercise 13.6 Hard

Two debts are held: $4,000 at 19 percent and $9,000 at 7 percent. Extra funds are available beyond the minimums. State the avalanche ordering and the snowball ordering, and explain the trade-off between them.

Solution

The avalanche method repays the 19 percent debt first, because it carries the higher rate; the snowball method also repays the $4,000 debt first, because it is the smaller balance. Here the two orderings coincide, so there is no conflict: the $4,000 debt is both the higher-rate and the smaller balance, and should be repaid first under either method.

The trade-off between the methods arises only when the highest-rate debt is not the smallest balance. In that case the avalanche minimises total interest, while the snowball produces an earlier first payoff at a modest cost in additional interest. The appropriate choice depends on whether the lower interest cost or the earlier completed payoff more reliably sustains repayment.

Building Wealth & Retirement

This is where every thread in the book ties together. Compounding (Chapter 3), diversification (Chapters 8 and 10), market efficiency (Chapter 11), and the budgeting discipline of Chapter 13 combine into a single, unremarkable, and highly effective plan for building wealth over a lifetime. There is no secret method — only a small number of well-understood actions, begun early and repeated for decades.

The most reliable millionaires in America are rarely the highest earners — they are teachers, engineers, and small-business owners who did a few ordinary things consistently: they spent less than they earned, invested the difference in low-cost diversified funds, used every available tax advantage and employer match, and then did the most difficult thing of all — remained invested, without action, for thirty years, through every decline and recovery. This chapter is that plan, built from the tools you now understand, and the arena lets you run your own version of it to retirement.

The wealth formula

Strip personal wealth-building to its core and it is four verbs: earn, save, invest, wait. Earn an income; save a portion of it (Chapter 13's gap); invest that savings in a diversified, low-cost portfolio (Chapters 10–11); and let compounding work for decades (Chapter 3). Each step is necessary and none is sufficient alone — a great income with no saving builds nothing, and diligent saving held as idle cash is eroded by inflation. It is the combination, sustained over time, that produces wealth. The single most powerful variable, as Chapter 3 showed, is time: the earlier you start, the more of the work compounding does for you.

Use the tax-advantaged accounts first

The government offers powerful incentives to save for retirement, and using them is among the most advantageous opportunities in personal finance. A 401(k) (through an employer) and an IRA (on your own) let your investments grow shielded from taxes. In a traditional account you deduct contributions now and pay tax on withdrawals later; in a Roth you pay tax now and withdraw tax-free in retirement. Either way, decades of compounding happen untaxed, which dramatically increases the final balance versus an ordinary taxable account.

A further benefit is the employer match, an unmatched guaranteed return. Many employers match your 401(k) contributions up to some percentage of salary. Not contributing enough to obtain the full match declines a guaranteed, immediate 100 percent return on that money. Asset allocation over a lifetime

What should you actually own inside these accounts? For most people, the answer is straightforward: a broad, low-cost index fund (Chapter 10), or a handful of them spanning stocks and bonds. The principal decision is the stock/bond mix, and it should shift with your time horizon. When retirement is decades away, you can hold mostly stocks — their volatility is of little consequence when the money will not be needed for thirty years, and their higher expected return compounds substantially. As retirement nears, you gradually shift toward bonds and cash, because a sharp decline in the year before retirement is a material danger rather than a remote one. This gradual shift is called a glide path, and “target-date” funds do it automatically.

The complete plan, in summary

Here is the entire book, turned into a personal plan. First, build a small emergency fund (a few months’ expenses) so that an unexpected expense does not require borrowing again at 22 percent. Second, pay off high-interest debt — a guaranteed return that no ordinary investment can match (Chapter 13). Third, contribute enough to obtain the full employer match — a guaranteed return. Fourth, invest steadily in low-cost, diversified funds inside tax-advantaged accounts, with a stock/bond mix suited to your horizon. Fifth, and most difficult, remain invested through market declines — do not sell during panics, do not pursue short-lived trends, and do not attempt to outperform a market that, as Chapter 11 showed, is very difficult to beat. Then let time and compounding do the rest.

The end of the book, the start of the plan

You began this book unable to say why a dollar today beats a dollar tomorrow. You can now value a bond, a company, and a portfolio; you understand risk, diversification, and the efficiency of markets; and you hold a concrete plan for your own financial life. None of it requires genius or luck — only understanding, discipline, and time, the one ingredient you have the most of right now. Start early, keep it simple, and let compounding, the central mechanism of Chapter 3, carry the process the rest of the way.

Exercise 14.1 Easy

You earn $50,000 and your employer matches 100% up to 4% of salary. If you contribute 4%, what is your total annual retirement contribution?

Solution

You contribute 4% × 50,000 = $2,000; the employer adds another $2,000. Total: $4,000 — the employer has immediately doubled the contribution before any investment return. Contributing less than 4 percent would forgo part of that $2,000.

Exercise 14.2 Medium

Why is the order “emergency fund → high-interest debt → capture the match → invest broadly” sensible? What could go wrong if you skip the emergency fund?

Solution

The order follows guaranteed returns and risk. High-interest debt payoff is a guaranteed ~20% return (Chapter 13); the employer match is a guaranteed ~100% return — both beat any market investment, so they come first. Broad investing comes after, for the long-run market return.

Skipping the emergency fund is dangerous because the first surprise expense — a car repair, a medical bill, a job loss — would force you to borrow at high rates or sell investments at a bad time, undoing your progress. The buffer keeps a temporary shock from becoming permanent 22% debt. It earns little, but it protects everything else.

Exercise 14.3 Hard

A colleague pulled all her retirement money into cash after a 30% market crash, “to be safe,” and plans to reinvest “once things calm down.” Using several ideas from this book, explain why this is likely a costly mistake.

Solution

Selling low locks in the loss (Chapter 8, 13): a paper loss becomes real only when you sell. By moving to cash after the fall, she converts a temporary decline into a permanent loss and gives up the recovery.

Market timing rarely works (Chapter 11): “once things calm down” means after prices have already risen — she'll likely buy back higher than she sold. The best days in the market cluster near the worst ones, and missing a handful of them devastates long-run returns. Efficient markets make the reinvestment point unknowable.

Her horizon makes volatility survivable (Chapter 3, 10): if retirement is years away, a 30% crash is noise that compounding will overwhelm — provided she stays invested. The disciplined move is to hold (and keep contributing, buying at lower prices). The single behavior that most separates successful investors from unsuccessful ones is not selling in panics — the defensive lesson of behavioral finance applied to the highest-stakes moment.
