Chapter 1 · Foundations

What Finance Is

📖 14 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 4 exercises + quiz

Finance is what you do when money and time collide. Every financial decision — by a person, a company, or a government — is a choice about giving up something now to get something later, or the reverse, in a world where the future is uncertain. That is the whole subject in one sentence. Everything else is detail, and this book is the detail.

Imagine you are handed $10,000 today, with one rule: you cannot spend it this year. You can put it under a mattress, lend it to a friend who promises $10,600 next year, buy a slice of a company, or hand it to a bank. A year from now you will be judged only on what you have. What should you do?

You cannot answer without weighing three things you feel instinctively but have probably never named: that a dollar next year is worth less to you than a dollar today, that a promise from a shaky friend is worth less than the same promise from a solid one, and that some bets pay more precisely because they might pay nothing. Those three instincts — time, risk, and the price of bearing them — are the load‑bearing walls of finance. By the last chapter you will reason about them with formulas; right now you already reason about them with your gut. This book connects the two.

The three questions

Strip away the jargon and all of finance is three recurring questions. A household faces them; so does Apple; so does a national treasury. They are simply asked at different scales.

The investment question
What should we own?

Of all the things you could put money into — a degree, a factory, a stock, a bond, a rental property — which are worth more than they cost? In corporate finance this is the capital budgeting decision; for you it is every choice to buy an asset instead of spending.

The financing question
How should we pay for it?

With your own money (equity) or with borrowed money (debt)? A firm chooses between issuing stock and issuing bonds; you choose between a down payment and a loan. This is the capital structure decision, and it changes who bears the risk and who keeps the reward.

The cash‑management question
How do we not run out of money in the meantime?

A profitable business can still fail if it cannot pay its bills next Tuesday. Managing the timing of cash in and cash out — working capital — is the unglamorous discipline that keeps the other two decisions from mattering.

Hold onto this frame. When you meet net present value in Chapter 7, it will be a tool for the investment question. When you meet the cost of capital in Chapter 9, it answers the financing question. The whole book is these three questions, asked more and more precisely.

The first force: money has a time value

Ask anyone whether they would rather have $1,000 today or $1,000 in a year, and they choose today — and they are right, for reasons deeper than impatience. A dollar today can be put to work: deposited, lent, or invested so that it becomes more than a dollar by next year. That earning power is called the time value of money, and the rate at which money grows over time is the interest rate, usually written $r$.

If a dollar grows at rate $r$ per year, then one dollar today becomes $1+r$ dollars next year. Two years out it becomes $(1+r)^2$, because the interest itself earns interest — the engine we will call compounding. Turned around: a dollar you will receive next year is worth only $1/(1+r)$ dollars to you today, because that smaller amount, invested now, would grow into the dollar. This one idea — that value depends on when a cash flow arrives — is the single most important idea in the book. We give it a full chapter next.

The two moves you will make a thousand times
Future value  →  FV = PV × (1 + r)n   // push a dollar forward in time
Present value  →  PV = FV / (1 + r)n   // pull a future dollar back to today

The plumbing: the financial system

Money has a time value because it can be put to work — but where? The answer is a vast machine for moving money from people who have more than they need right now (savers) to people who can do something productive with it (borrowers and firms). That machine is the financial system, and it has three moving parts.

Financial markets

Places — mostly electronic now — where financial assets are bought and sold. The stock market trades ownership shares of companies; the bond market, far larger, trades loans. Markets set prices, and a price is just the crowd’s answer to “what is this future stream of cash worth today?”

Financial intermediaries

Institutions that stand between saver and borrower: banks, insurers, pension funds, mutual funds. You deposit $5,000; the bank lends it to a business; you never meet the business. Intermediaries exist because they are better than you at two hard jobs — judging who will repay, and pooling many small savers so that one borrower’s default does not sink any single lender.

The instruments themselves

The contracts that get traded: a stock is a share of ownership and its uncertain profits; a bond is a promise to repay a loan on a schedule; a derivative is a side‑bet whose value depends on something else. Chapters 5, 6, and 12 take these apart one by one. For now, notice what they share: every one of them is a claim on future cash, which is why valuing future cash is the skill the whole book is built to teach.

The one job of the whole system

Channel savings to their most productive use, and price the risk of doing so. When the system works, a good idea with no money finds money with no ideas. When it breaks — 2008 — the channel clogs, and the damage spreads far beyond finance because everything real is financed.

Real assets and financial assets

It helps to keep two categories straight. Real assets are the productive things themselves — factories, farmland, patents, an education, a delivery truck. They generate the economy’s actual output. Financial assets — stocks, bonds, loans — are claims on the income that real assets produce. A share of a bakery is not the ovens; it is the right to a slice of what the ovens earn.

This distinction quietly resolves a lot of confusion. When the stock market falls, no ovens vanish; the market has only revised its estimate of what the ovens will earn. Financial assets are society’s ledger for dividing up the output of its real assets — and finance, as a field, is largely the art of pricing that ledger correctly.

The second force: risk

If time were the only force, finance would be arithmetic. The complication — and the reason it is a real subject — is that the future is uncertain. The friend might not repay. The company might not profit. The word for this is risk, and finance takes a specific, initially surprising stance on it: risk is not merely to be avoided, it is to be priced.

Because most people dislike uncertainty, they must be paid to bear it. That payment is the risk premium — the extra expected return a risky investment must offer over a safe one to attract any takers. It is why stocks have historically returned more than government bonds, and why a loan to a shaky borrower carries a higher interest rate than a loan to a solid one. Risk and return are joined at the hip, and Chapters 8 through 12 are essentially one long study of that joint.

The two forces, together

Every price in finance is an answer to one question: what is this stream of future cash worth today, given how long we must wait for it and how uncertain it is? Waiting is handled by discounting for time; uncertainty is handled by demanding a risk premium. Learn to answer that question and you can value anything — a bond, a business, a lottery, a lease, your own degree.

Why this matters to you

You will make a handful of financial decisions in your life that dwarf all the others: how much to borrow for school, whether and when to buy a home, how to invest for a retirement forty years away, how much debt to carry and at what rate. Each is an instance of the three questions, subject to the two forces. People who understand the machinery make these decisions with quiet confidence; people who do not pay a lifelong tax in bad rates, missed compounding, and avoidable risk.

This is not a course in getting rich, and it will not tell you which stock to buy. It is a course in thinking clearly about money and time under uncertainty — the one skill underneath all the specific decisions. The personal‑finance chapters at the end (13 and 14) turn the theory directly on your own life; everything before them builds the tools.

A look ahead

  • Chapters 2–3 make the time value of money exact: present value, future value, and the quiet power of compounding.
  • Chapters 4–6 use it to value real things — loans and mortgages, bonds, and whole companies.
  • Chapters 7–9 are corporate finance: choosing investments (NPV), measuring risk and return, and paying for it all (the cost of capital).
  • Chapters 10–12 turn to markets and investing: diversification, whether markets can be beaten, and options.
  • Chapters 13–14 bring it home to budgeting, debt, taxes, and building your own wealth.

You are ready for Chapter 2, where the one idea in this chapter — that value depends on timing — becomes a precise and surprisingly powerful tool.

Where this came from

The time value of money is old. Medieval merchants and the mathematician Fibonacci (1202) computed present values to compare trades; Renaissance bankers priced loans with compound interest tables. But finance became a rigorous, mathematical field only in the twentieth century: Irving Fisher formalized interest and capital in the 1930s, Harry Markowitz put risk on a mathematical footing in 1952, and by the 1970s the pricing of options had become precise enough to win Nobel Prizes. This book walks that same path — intuition first, then the mathematics that made it exact — in a single course.

Exercise 1.1 Easy

For each item, say whether it is a real asset or a financial asset: (a) a coffee‑shop’s espresso machine, (b) 50 shares of that coffee shop’s stock, (c) a $20,000 car loan you took out, from the bank’s point of view, (d) a college degree.

Solution

(a) Real — a productive physical thing. (b) Financial — a claim on the shop’s future profits. (c) Financial — to the bank the loan is an asset: a claim on your future repayments. (d) Real — human capital; it raises your future productive earning power, even though you cannot sell it.

Note the pattern: real assets produce income; financial assets are claims on it. The same contract (the loan) is a liability to you and an asset to the bank — every financial asset is somebody else’s obligation.

Exercise 1.2 Easy

The interest rate is 6%. (a) What is $500 today worth in one year? (b) What is $500 to be received in one year worth today? Explain in one sentence why the two answers differ.

Solution

(a) Future value: 500 × 1.06 = $530. (b) Present value: 500 / 1.06 = $471.70.

They differ because moving money forward in time adds interest (multiply by 1+r), while moving it backward removes the interest it would have earned (divide by 1+r). Today’s $471.70, invested at 6%, becomes exactly $500 next year — which is what “present value” means.

Exercise 1.3 Medium

A startup with a brilliant product has no cash; a retiree has $50,000 in savings and no ideas. Describe, using the three parts of the financial system, one path by which the retiree’s savings could end up funding the startup — and name the risk the retiree is being paid to bear.

Solution

One path: the retiree buys shares of a venture‑capital or mutual fund (an intermediary) through the market; the fund uses pooled money to buy the startup’s stock (the instrument), delivering the savings to the firm. Alternatively the retiree buys a bond the startup issues.

The risk being priced is that the startup fails and the investment is lost. The retiree accepts that risk only in exchange for a risk premium — an expected return above what a safe government bond would pay. The intermediary provides value by assessing which startups merit investment and by pooling many of them so that no single failure is ruinous.

Exercise 1.4 Hard

“When the stock market crashes, the country becomes poorer by exactly the amount of market value that disappeared.” Using the real‑asset / financial‑asset distinction, explain what is right and what is wrong about this claim.

Solution

Wrong, in an immediate physical sense: a crash destroys no real assets. The factories, workers, and patents are all still there the morning after. What changed is the market’s estimate of the future income those real assets will produce — a repricing of financial claims, not a disappearance of productive capacity.

Right, in a forward‑looking sense: the price drop usually reflects genuinely worse expectations about future output (a coming recession, say). And there are real feedback effects — a crash can make firms and households cut spending (a wealth effect) and make financing harder to get, which can actually reduce future real output.

So the crash is better read as a signal and an amplifier of expected real losses than as the loss itself. The distinction between the ledger (financial assets) and the thing being tallied (real assets) is exactly what keeps this straight.

The Time Value of Money

📖 18 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 4 exercises + quiz

In Chapter 1 we said a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. This chapter turns that instinct into a tool sharp enough to compare any two streams of cash, arriving at any times, on a single common ruler: their value today. Master this one move — discounting — and you can value a bond, a business, a mortgage, or a lottery jackpot. Everything after this chapter is an application of it.

You win the lottery. The clerk offers a choice: $1,000,000 in cash right now, or $50,000 a year for the next 30 years — $1,500,000 in total. The second pile is half a million dollars bigger. Most people take it. Most people are wrong, and by the end of this chapter you will be able to say exactly why, and by how much.

The trick is that the $1.5M is an illusion of addition: it sums dollars that arrive in different years as if they were the same thing. They are not. A dollar arriving in year 30 is worth far less to you today than a dollar in your hand now, because the dollar in hand can be invested and grow for 30 years. To compare the two offers honestly, we must drag every future payment back to the present — and once we do, the “bigger” pile often shrinks below the cash.

Pushing money forward: future value

Start with the easy direction. Put $PV$ dollars in an account earning $r$ per year. After one year you have $PV(1+r)$. Leave it another year and that whole balance grows again, so you have $PV(1+r)^2$ — the interest earned in year 1 itself earns interest in year 2. After $n$ years:

Future value of a single amount
FV = PV × (1 + r)n   // each year multiplies the whole balance by (1+r)

The exponent is where the magic hides. Growth is not linear (add $r$ each year) but exponential (multiply by $1+r$ each year), and over decades the difference is enormous. Chapter 3 is devoted to it. Here we need the reverse direction.

Pulling money back: present value

Now flip the question. Someone promises you a fixed amount $FV$ at a future date. What is that promise worth to you today? It must be the amount that, invested now at rate $r$, would grow into exactly $FV$ by then. Solve the future‑value formula for $PV$:

Present value of a single future amount
PV = FV / (1 + r)n   // divide out the growth the money would have earned

This operation — converting a future dollar into its worth today — is called discounting, and $r$ in this role is the discount rate. The factor $1/(1+r)^n$ is the discount factor; it is always less than one for positive rates, which is just the mathematics saying “future money is worth less.” The farther away the cash flow (larger $n$) or the higher the rate, the smaller the discount factor, and the less a future dollar is worth now.

Definition
Present value (PV)

The value today of a cash flow (or a whole stream of cash flows) that will be received in the future, obtained by discounting each future amount at the appropriate rate. Present value is the universal common denominator of finance: it lets us add and compare cash flows that arrive at different times.

Worked example
A promise three years out

A trustworthy borrower will pay you $5,000 in exactly 3 years. Comparable safe investments earn 6%. What is the promise worth today?

PV = 5000 / (1.06)3 = 5000 / 1.1910 = $4,198.10

So you should be willing to pay up to $4,198.10 for this promise, and no more — because $4,198.10 invested at 6% grows to exactly $5,000 in three years. If someone offers to sell you the promise for $3,900, take it: you are paying less than it is worth.

Valuing a whole stream: PV is additive

Real investments rarely pay once. A bond pays interest every year and principal at the end; a business throws off cash for decades. The key fact that makes these tractable is beautifully simple: present values add up. The value today of a stream of cash flows is just the sum of the present values of its individual pieces. If a project pays $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n$ at the end of years $1$ through $n$:

PV = C1/(1+r) + C2/(1+r)2 + … + Cn/(1+r)n

Each term drags one future payment back to today; the sum is what the whole stream is worth now. This single equation, in one form or another, is behind every valuation in this book. Where does the discount rate come from?

The formula needs a number for $r$, and choosing it well is most of the art of valuation. The discount rate is fundamentally an opportunity cost: the return you could earn on your next‑best alternative investment of similar risk. If safe government bonds yield 4%, then a safe future cash flow should be discounted at 4% — because 4% is what you give up by tying your money in this promise instead.

Risk raises it. A cash flow you are unsure of should be discounted at a higher rate than a certain one, because your next‑best alternative of comparable risk earns more (recall the risk premium from Chapter 1). A risky corporate cash flow might be discounted at 10% while a Treasury payment is discounted at 4%. So the discount rate quietly encodes both forces of finance at once: the pure time value (the safe rate) plus a premium for the risk. Chapters 8 and 9 build the machinery that pins down the right premium; for now, hold the intuition: riskier future cash is discounted harder.

The master recipe

To value anything: (1) forecast its future cash flows, (2) choose a discount rate that reflects their risk, (3) discount each flow back to today, and (4) add them up. Every valuation in this book — bonds, stocks, whole companies, your own degree — is a special case of these four steps. The differences are only in how you forecast the cash flows and pick the rate.

From present value to a decision: NPV

Present value tells you what a stream of future cash is worth. To decide whether to buy it, compare that worth to the price. The difference is net present value:

NPV = (PV of everything it pays you) − (what it costs today)

If NPV is positive, the thing is worth more than it costs — accept it. If negative, reject it. That is the entire logic of the investment decision, and the live sheet above computes it for you. Chapter 7 makes NPV the centerpiece of corporate finance; you have just met it three chapters early, because it falls straight out of discounting.

And the lottery? Discount the 30 annual $50,000 payments at a modest 6% and their present value is about $688,000 — well under the $1,000,000 cash. The “bigger” $1.5M pile is worth less today than the smaller cash offer. Take the million. (You now have the tools to check this yourself; Exercise 2.4 walks you through it.)

Exercise 2.1 Easy

The discount rate is 5%. (a) What is $2,000 received in 4 years worth today? (b) What is $2,000 today worth in 4 years?

Solution

(a) PV = 2000 / 1.054 = 2000 / 1.2155 = $1,645.40.

Exercise 2.2 Medium

A project pays $1,000 at the end of year 1 and $2,000 at the end of year 2. The discount rate is 10%. What is the present value of the stream?

Solution

Discount each flow and add (PV is additive):

PV = 1000/1.10 + 2000/1.102 = 909.09 + 1652.89 = $2,561.98.

Notice the year‑2 flow, though twice as large, is discounted twice, so it is not simply worth twice the year‑1 flow. Timing matters as much as size.

Exercise 2.4 Hard

Return to the lottery. Approximate the present value of $50,000 per year for 30 years at 6% by reasoning about it, then explain in words why the answer is so far below the $1,500,000 headline total. (You may use that the PV of $1 per year for 30 years at 6% is about 13.76.)

Solution

PV ≈ 50,000 × 13.76 = $688,000 — below the $1,000,000 cash offer, so the cash wins.

The headline $1.5M adds 30 payments as if a dollar in year 30 equals a dollar today. But the year‑30 payment is discounted by 1.0630 ≈ 5.74, so it is worth only about $8,700 today, not $50,000. Averaged across all 30 years, each $50,000 payment is worth well under half its face value in present terms. Summing face values ignores the time value of money — exactly the error this chapter exists to cure. (The factor 13.76 is an annuity factor, the shortcut we build in Chapter 4.)

Where this came from

Discounting is old arithmetic put to new use. Leonardo of Pisa (“Fibonacci”), in Liber Abaci (1202), worked present‑value problems to compare business deals across time. But the idea became the organizing principle of finance only in 1930, when Yale economist Irving Fisher, in The Theory of Interest, argued that the value of any asset is the present value of the income it will produce — and that investment decisions should be made by comparing present values. Every valuation model in this book is a descendant of that claim.

Compounding & Growth

📖 16 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 4 exercises + quiz

Compounding is the quiet engine underneath every number in this book. It is also the most underestimated force in personal finance, because human intuition is linear and compounding is not. This chapter makes the engine precise — how compounding frequency, stated rates, and time interact — and then shows you the single most important practical consequence: when it comes to building wealth, starting early beats saving more.

Two twins, Ana and Ben, each eventually save $200 a month at a 7% return. Ana starts at 25 and stops contributing at 35 — ten years, $24,000 of her own money in total. Ben waits, starts at 35, and contributes every month until 65 — thirty years, $72,000 of his own money. Ben puts in three times as much, for three times as long. At 65, who has more?

Ana does — by a wide margin. Her $24,000, given a forty‑year head start to compound, outgrows Ben's $72,000. That result feels impossible until you understand compounding, and it becomes obvious once you do. It is the reason this chapter sits so early in the book, and the reason the companion arena lets you run exactly this race yourself.

Linear growth vs. exponential growth

If money earned simple interest, only your original principal would earn, and growth would be a straight line: $1,000 at 10% simple interest gains $100 every year, forever. But real accounts pay compound interest: each period's interest is added to the balance and then itself earns interest. Growth becomes exponential — the curve bends upward, gently at first, then dramatically.

Over one year the difference is trivial. Over forty, it is staggering: $1,000 at 10% simple interest becomes $5,000 after 40 years, but at 10% compounded it becomes about $45,000 — nine times as much, from the same rate. The entire gap is interest earning interest. This is why Einstein (probably apocryphally) is said to have called compound interest the eighth wonder of the world, and why the rest of this section is about pinning it down exactly.

How often interest compounds: APR vs. EAR

Here intuition needs a guardrail, because rates are quoted in a slippery way. A credit card advertises “18% APR”; a savings account, “2% compounded monthly.” The APR (annual percentage rate) is just the periodic rate times the number of periods — a stated, nominal rate that ignores compounding within the year. The number you actually earn or pay is the EAR (effective annual rate), which accounts for compounding.

From a stated APR to the rate you truly pay
EAR = (1 + APR/m)m − 1   // m = compounding periods per year

An 18% APR credit card compounding monthly has an EAR of $(1 + 0.18/12)^{12} - 1 = 19.56\%$ — you truly pay almost 20%, not 18%. The more frequently interest compounds, the more the EAR exceeds the APR. Push the frequency to its limit — compounding every instant — and you get continuous compounding, where the formula becomes $FV = PV \cdot e^{rn}$. The Rule of 72: compounding in your head

You will not always have a calculator, and a remarkably good shortcut exists for the question “how long until my money doubles?” Divide 72 by the interest rate in percent:

Years to double ≈ 72 / (interest rate in %)

At 8%, money doubles in about $72/8 = 9$ years; at 6%, about 12 years; at 3%, about 24. The rule is an approximation (it comes from the logarithm of 2), but it is accurate to within a few months for the rates you will meet, and it makes the power of higher returns visceral: the difference between a 3% and a 9% return is not “three times more” — it is doubling every 8 years instead of every 24, which over a career is the difference between doubling five times and doubling once.

Why early beats more

Because balances double on a fixed clock set by the rate, the dollars you invest earliest get the most doublings. A dollar invested at 25 and left to 65 at 7% doubles roughly every 10 years — four doublings, turning $1 into about $16. The same dollar invested at 45 gets only two doublings, reaching about $4. Time in the market, not timing or even amount, is what compounding rewards. This is the most valuable single sentence in the book for your own finances.

Real vs. nominal: don't forget inflation

One honesty check before we celebrate compounding too much. The returns above are nominal — measured in dollars. But dollars themselves lose purchasing power to inflation. What you actually care about is the real return: growth in what your money can buy. A good approximation is simply to subtract:

real return ≈ nominal return − inflation rate

Earn 7% while inflation runs 3%, and your purchasing power grows at about 4%. This matters enormously over long horizons: a retirement plan that ignores inflation will badly overstate what your future balance can buy. We will keep nominal and real straight throughout — especially in the personal finance chapters — but the rule to remember now is that compounding works on inflation too, silently eroding cash left idle, which is itself an argument for investing rather than hoarding.

Where this came from

The mathematics of compound interest predates modern finance by centuries — Jacob Bernoulli discovered the constant e in 1683 precisely by studying continuous compounding. The Rule of 72 appears in Luca Pacioli's 1494 accounting treatise, the same book that codified double‑entry bookkeeping. What is modern is the emphasis: the recognition, formalized by twentieth‑century economists and popularized by index‑fund pioneers like John Bogle, that for ordinary savers time in the market and low costs dominate almost everything else.

Annuities, Loans & Mortgages

📖 20 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Most of the cash flows in real life are not one-off amounts but streams of equal payments: a car loan, a mortgage, a monthly savings deposit, a pension. Discounting each one separately would be tedious, so finance gives us a shortcut — the annuity formula — that collapses a whole stream into a single present value. This chapter turns that shortcut into the loan and mortgage math you will use for the rest of your life.

You want a $30,000 car. The dealer offers “$560 a month for 60 months.” That is $33,600 in total payments — but is it a good deal? To answer, you need to run the arithmetic backward: what interest rate turns $30,000 today into 60 payments of $560? By the end of this chapter you will compute that rate, see how much of each payment is interest, and know exactly what you are agreeing to when you sign.

The annuity: a stream of equal payments

An annuity is a series of equal cash flows $C$ paid at regular intervals for $n$ periods. Because each payment is the same, the sum of their present values has a clean closed form:

Present value of an annuity
PV = C × [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ] / r   // C = payment, r = rate per period, n = number of payments

The bracketed term is the annuity factor — the present value of $1 paid every period for $n$ periods. Multiply any payment by it and you get the whole stream's value today. (This is the mysterious “13.76” from the lottery in Chapter 2: the annuity factor for 30 years at 6%.) It is worth understanding why it works: it is just the sum of the individual discount factors $1/(1+r) + 1/(1+r)^2 + \dots$, which is a geometric series that collapses to the formula above.

Perpetuities: payments forever

Let the number of payments go to infinity and the annuity formula simplifies beautifully. A perpetuity — an equal payment that never ends — is worth simply:

PV = C / r

A payment of $100 a year forever, at a 5% discount rate, is worth $100 / 0.05 = $2,000 today. That a stream lasting forever has a finite value is one of the small wonders of discounting: payments far in the future are discounted so heavily that the infinite tail adds almost nothing. Perpetuities are not just a curiosity — they are the backbone of stock valuation in Chapter 6, where a company is often modeled as a growing perpetuity of cash.

Loans and amortization

A loan is an annuity viewed from the borrower's side. The lender hands you a present value (the loan amount $P$) today; you repay it with a stream of equal payments. Setting the present value of your payments equal to the loan and solving for the payment gives:

The loan payment formula
Payment = P × r / [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ]   // r and n in the payment period (months, usually)

Each payment does two jobs: it pays the interest that accrued that period, and it chips away at the principal. Early on, the balance is large, so most of the payment is interest and little goes to principal. As the balance falls, the split flips. This process is called amortization, and it is why the first years of a mortgage build so little equity. Drive the live amortizer below and watch the balance curve bend.

Bonds & Interest Rates

📖 20 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

A bond is the simplest security to value, because its cash flows are written into the contract: fixed coupons on a schedule, then the face value at the end. Valuing one is pure Chapter 2 — discount the cash flows and add them up. What makes bonds fascinating is the mirror relationship at their core: when interest rates rise, bond prices fall, and understanding exactly why unlocks the entire fixed-income market, the largest financial market on earth.

You buy a bond that pays $50 a year and returns $1,000 at maturity. The next morning, the Federal Reserve raises rates and newly issued bonds now pay $70. Suddenly no one wants your $50 bond at the price you paid — why accept $50 when $70 is on offer? Your bond's price drops until its $50 payments represent the same deal as everyone else's $70. You did nothing, held a “safe” government bond, and still lost money. That is interest-rate risk, and this chapter makes it precise.

Anatomy of a bond

A bond is a loan you make to a government or company, packaged as a tradable security. Three numbers define it: the face value (or par, usually $1,000) repaid at the end; the coupon rate, which fixes the annual interest payment as a percentage of face; and the maturity, the number of years until the face value is repaid. A “5% coupon, 10-year, $1,000 bond” pays $50 every year for 10 years, then $1,000.

Definition
Yield to maturity (YTM)

The single discount rate that makes the present value of a bond's cash flows equal to its market price — in other words, the annual return you earn if you buy the bond now and hold it to maturity. YTM is the bond market's version of an interest rate, and it is what people mean when they say “the 10-year yield.”

Pricing a bond is just present value

A bond's price is the present value of its coupons (an annuity) plus the present value of its face value (a single amount), both discounted at the market yield $y$:

Bond price
Price = C × [1 − (1+y)−n] / y   +   F / (1+y)n   // coupons (annuity) + face value (lump sum)

The mirror: prices and yields move opposite

Here is the single most important fact in fixed income: bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. The reason is baked into the pricing formula — the yield sits in the denominator, so a larger yield means a smaller price. But the intuition is cleaner: a bond's coupon is fixed. If market rates rise, that fixed coupon looks stingy next to newly issued bonds, so the old bond's price must fall until its yield matches the new market rate. If rates fall, the old bond's above-market coupon becomes desirable, and its price rises.

This gives us three cases, which the pricer labels for you. When the coupon rate equals the market yield, the bond trades at par (price = face). When the coupon exceeds the yield, buyers bid the price above face — a premium bond. When the coupon is below the yield, the price sinks below face — a discount bond.

Interest-rate risk and duration

Not all bonds react equally to a rate change. A bond that matures next year barely moves when yields rise — you get your money back soon and reinvest at the new rate. A 30-year bond, whose cash flows are locked in for decades, swings violently. The general rule: longer-maturity bonds are more sensitive to interest-rate changes. The precise measure of this sensitivity is called duration — roughly, the percentage a bond's price falls for each one-percentage-point rise in yield.

You do not need the duration formula for this course, but you need the intuition, because it explains real events. When central banks raised rates sharply in 2022, long-term bond funds — supposedly the “safe” part of a portfolio — fell 20% or more, precisely because their long duration made them acutely sensitive. “Safe from default” is not the same as “safe from price swings.” Slide the pricer's maturity to 30 years and then move the yield to see this magnified sensitivity yourself.

Two kinds of bond risk

Default risk is the chance the issuer fails to pay — low for governments, higher for shaky companies, and compensated by a higher yield. Interest-rate risk is the chance rates move and reprice your bond before maturity — present even for perfectly safe government bonds. A U.S. Treasury has almost no default risk but plenty of interest-rate risk.

Valuing a Company

📖 32 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 6 exercises + quiz

A bond tells you its cash flows; a company makes you guess them. That gap is the central difficulty in valuing a business, and the source of its analytical interest. The machinery, though, is the one you already own: a company is worth the present value of the cash it will hand its owners over its lifetime. This chapter introduces the apparatus a professional analyst uses to estimate that figure: the financial statements that record what a firm has done, the ratios that convert those statements into judgment, and three valuation methods that translate the result into a price per share.

In 2000, the market valued a profitless pet-supply website at more than a billion dollars; eighteen months later it was worth nothing. In 2004, a search company went public at a price many analysts called indefensible; two decades later that price appears, in hindsight, conspicuously low. Both numbers were claims about future cash, and both were produced by serious people using the same tools you are about to learn. The tools did not fail. What separated the two outcomes was the quality of the assumptions fed into them. A valuation model does not remove uncertainty about the future; it forces you to state your assumptions in the open, where they can be examined, defended, and—when necessary—proven wrong for a reason rather than by accident.

A share is a claim on future cash

When you buy a share, you buy a fractional claim on every dollar the company will ever distribute to its owners: dividends paid along the way, plus whatever a buyer will one day pay for the share, which is itself a claim on the dividends still to come. Carried to its conclusion, the terminal sale price drops out of the arithmetic, leaving a precise statement of fundamental value: a stock is worth the present value of the future cash it returns to its owner. This is the principle of Chapter 2 applied to the most difficult asset in the book. The three valuation methods below do not disagree about this principle. They differ only in how they estimate the cash and what rate they discount it at.

Before any of them can be used, though, you need to read the record of what the company has actually done. That record is the set of financial statements, and learning to read them is a step that introductory treatments often compress. It is treated here deliberately.

The three financial statements

A public company reports its activity in three linked statements. Each answers a different question, and valuation draws on all three.

The income statement
Did the firm earn a profit over the period?

A flow, measured over a quarter or a year. It begins with revenue, subtracts the cost of goods sold to reach gross profit, subtracts operating expenses to reach operating income (often called EBIT, earnings before interest and taxes), then subtracts interest and taxes to reach net income, the “bottom line” that belongs to shareholders.

The balance sheet
What does the firm own and owe at a moment in time?

A snapshot, taken on the last day of the period. It obeys one identity: assets = liabilities + shareholders’ equity. Everything the firm controls is financed either by someone it owes (a liability) or by its owners (equity). Equity is therefore a residual: what would be left for owners if every asset were sold at its book value and every debt repaid.

The statement of cash flows
Where did the actual cash come from and go?

Net income is an accounting figure, shaped by judgment calls about depreciation, accruals, and timing. The cash-flow statement strips those away and tracks real cash across three activities: operating, investing, and financing. It is the bridge from reported profit to the free cash flow that valuation ultimately runs on.

Why profit is not cash

A firm can report a healthy profit and still be unable to pay its suppliers, because a sale booked on credit raises net income the day it is made but brings in no cash until the customer pays. The reverse happens too: a large upfront investment drains cash without touching that year’s profit, because the cost is spread over the asset’s life as depreciation. Valuation depends on cash, not accounting profit, and the gap between the two is a frequent source of analytical error.

Financial ratios: turning statements into judgment

Raw statement figures are hard to interpret on their own. Is $105m of net income good? The question has no answer until you ask “relative to what?” — relative to the revenue that produced it, the assets employed to earn it, or the equity the owners committed. A financial ratio is exactly that comparison: one statement figure divided by another, chosen so the result means something. Ratios are the vocabulary analysts use to compare a firm with its own past, with its competitors, and with the price the market is asking. They fall into five families.

Profitability: how much of each dollar is kept

Profitability ratios measure how efficiently a firm converts activity into earnings. The margin ratios are drawn directly from successive lines of the income statement: gross, operating, and net margin each divide a profit line by revenue. For Northwind, gross margin is $420/1{,}200 = 35\%$, operating margin is $170/1{,}200 \approx 14.2\%$, and net margin is $105/1{,}200 \approx 8.8\%$. Two return ratios go further by comparing profit to the capital used to generate it: return on assets (ROA = net income / total assets = $105/1{,}000 = 10.5\%$) and return on equity (ROE = net income / equity = $105/500 = 21\%$).

The gap between ROA and ROE is not incidental; it reflects the firm’s use of leverage, and the DuPont identity makes that mechanism explicit by decomposing ROE into three drivers:

The DuPont decomposition of ROE
ROE = (Net income / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
// net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier

For Northwind: $0.088 \times 1.20 \times 2.0 = 0.21$, which is the 21% ROE exactly. The decomposition tells you why a firm earns what it earns. A high ROE built on a wide net margin (a luxury brand) differs materially from one built on rapid asset turnover (a discount grocer) or one built on substantial borrowing (the equity multiplier of 2.0 indicates that half of Northwind’s assets are debt-financed). Two firms can report an identical 21% ROE while facing entirely different risks, and the DuPont decomposition is the means of distinguishing them.

Liquidity: can the firm pay its near-term bills?

Liquidity ratios ask whether short-term assets cover short-term obligations. The current ratio is current assets over current liabilities: $400/200 = 2.0$, meaning Northwind has two dollars of near-term assets for every dollar of near-term debt. The quick ratio (or acid test) strips out inventory, which can be slow to sell: $(400-160)/200 = 1.2$. The cash ratio, strictest of all, counts only cash: $90/200 = 0.45$. A higher ratio indicates greater safety, but a current ratio well above the industry norm may also indicate idle cash or excess inventory that the firm has not deployed productively.

Leverage and solvency: how much debt, and can it be serviced?

Where liquidity is about next month, solvency is about survival. The debt-to-equity ratio compares borrowed money to owners’ money; using interest-bearing debt, Northwind’s is $(80+300)/500 = 0.76$. More revealing is the interest coverage ratio (times interest earned), which asks how comfortably operating income covers the interest bill: $170/30 \approx 5.7\times$. A coverage of 5.7 means earnings could fall substantially before the firm struggled to pay its lenders; a coverage near 1.0 is a warning. Lenders and credit-rating agencies watch net debt to EBITDA closely: here net debt is total debt minus cash, $380-90=290$, so net-debt/EBITDA is $290/230 \approx 1.3\times$, a conservative figure.

Efficiency: how productively are the assets used?

Efficiency (or activity) ratios measure how quickly a firm turns its assets into sales and cash. Asset turnover is revenue over assets: $1{,}200/1{,}000 = 1.2$, the same figure that appeared in DuPont. Three cycle ratios track working capital. Days inventory is $365 \div (780/160) \approx 75$ days; days sales outstanding (how long customers take to pay) is $365 \div (1{,}200/150) \approx 46$ days; days payable (how long the firm takes to pay suppliers) is $365 \div (780/120) \approx 56$ days. Combine them into the cash conversion cycle — days inventory plus days receivable minus days payable, here $75 + 46 - 56 = 65$ days — the number of days the firm’s own cash is tied up before a sale finally returns it. A shorter cycle is preferable; a negative cycle, which some large retailers achieve, means that suppliers effectively finance the firm’s operations.

Valuation multiples: the price the market assigns

The first four families describe the business. The fifth compares the business to its price, and these are the ratios that connect statement analysis to the valuation methods that follow. The price-to-earnings ratio is price over earnings per share. Northwind’s EPS is $105/60 = \$1.75$, so its P/E is $28/1.75 = 16$: investors pay $16 for each dollar of current annual earnings. The price-to-book ratio compares price to accounting net worth per share ($500/60 = \$8.33$), giving $28/8.33 \approx 3.4$. The dividend yield is dividend per share over price, $0.70/28 = 2.5\%$.

Equity multiples like P/E are distorted by how a firm is financed, because earnings are measured after interest. To compare firms with different debt loads on equal footing, analysts turn to enterprise value — the value of the whole business to all its funders — defined as market capitalization plus net debt: $1{,}680 + 290 = \$1{,}970m$. The headline multiple is EV/EBITDA, here $1{,}970/230 \approx 8.6\times$, which is capital-structure neutral and therefore the standard tool for comparing acquisition targets.

A ratio is a question, not an answer

No ratio is meaningful in isolation. A P/E of 16 is cheap for a fast grower and expensive for a shrinking one; a current ratio of 2.0 is prudent for a manufacturer and wastefully high for a supermarket. Ratios become informative only through comparison — against the same firm over several years (trend), against direct competitors (cross-section), and against the firm’s own stated expectations. And because every ratio is built from accounting figures, it inherits every accounting choice: different depreciation schedules or inventory methods can make two identical businesses look different. Ratios should be used to form questions — why a margin is declining, why a firm trades below its peers — which the analyst then investigates.

Method 1: the dividend discount model

With the statements read, we can value the cash they imply. The simplest case is a firm that returns cash directly and predictably. If it pays a dividend that grows at a steady rate $g$ forever, its stock is a growing perpetuity, and the growing-perpetuity formula from Chapter 4 collapses into the elegant Gordon growth model:

Gordon growth (dividend discount) model
Price = D1 / (r − g)   // D₁ = next year's dividend, r = required return, g = growth rate

A stock about to pay a $2 dividend, growing 4% a year, held by investors who require a 9% return, is worth $2/(0.09-0.04) = \$40$. The formula holds only when $r > g$: a company that grew faster than its discount rate forever would be worth infinity, which no company is. The model suits stable, cash-returning businesses — utilities, mature consumer brands — and strains badly on young firms that pay nothing. Change the inputs and watch the price move:

Method 2: discounted cash flow (DCF)

Most interesting companies do not pay steady dividends; they reinvest to grow, and their payout today understates their value. For these we value the whole business by its free cash flow — the cash left after operating costs, taxes, and the investment needed to sustain growth. A DCF has three steps: project free cash flow explicitly for several years, capture everything after the forecast horizon in a single terminal value (usually a growing perpetuity), and discount both back at the company’s cost of capital, which Chapter 9 derives. Subtract net debt from the resulting enterprise value, divide by shares outstanding, and you have an intrinsic value per share to set against the market price.

A compact illustration makes the shape concrete. Suppose a firm’s free cash flow next year is $100m, growing 8% for five years, after which it settles to 3% growth forever; the cost of capital is 10%. The five explicit cash flows are discounted one by one. The terminal value, computed at the end of year five as $FCF_6/(r-g) = FCF_6/(0.10-0.03)$, is then itself discounted back five years. In a realistic model that terminal value often accounts for sixty to eighty percent of the total — which is precisely why DCF demands discipline.

DCF is the principal tool of professional valuation, and it is the model you will build in the arena. Its power is also its hazard: the answer depends on a discount rate and a long-run growth rate that can only be estimated, and the terminal value magnifies both. A careful analyst never reports a single number; they run the model across a range of assumptions and report a valuation band.

Method 3: multiples, the quick comparison

When a rapid check is wanted rather than a full model, the valuation multiples from the ratio dashboard serve that purpose. Multiples value a company by comparison: if similar firms trade at 20 times earnings (a P/E of 20) and this firm earns $5 per share, a first estimate of its value is $20 \times 5 = \$100$ per share. The same logic runs on EV/EBITDA for the whole firm, on price-to-book for banks, on EV/sales for companies not yet profitable. Multiples are rapid, market-based, and easily communicated, but they embed the market’s prevailing sentiment toward the entire sector. “Fairly valued relative to other overpriced stocks” is a genuine hazard, which is why a multiple serves as a cross-check rather than a conclusion.

The same idea, three lenses

Dividends, discounted free cash flow, and multiples are three windows onto one question: what is a claim on this company’s future cash worth today? A careful analyst triangulates. When a DCF, a dividend model, and comparable multiples all cluster near $80, that agreement is far stronger than any single method alone. When they diverge sharply, the disagreement is itself the most useful output, because it points straight to the one assumption the whole valuation turns on.

Exercise 6.6 Hard

The result is exquisitely sensitive to two of the least certain inputs in the whole model. This is not a flaw to conceal but a fact to manage: run the model across a range of discount and growth assumptions and report a valuation band, never a single false-precision figure. The arena is built to let you feel this sensitivity directly.

Where this came from

Ratio analysis grew out of lending. In the early twentieth century, bankers deciding whether to extend credit developed the current ratio and interest coverage as quick tests of whether a borrower could repay. The DuPont Company’s own finance staff built the ROE decomposition in the 1910s to manage a sprawling industrial empire, and it still bears the firm’s name. Benjamin Graham and David Dodd turned ratios into a philosophy of investing in Security Analysis (1934), insisting that price be judged against demonstrable value. Discounted cash flow, though its roots reach back to compound-interest tables, became the standard corporate tool only after the mathematics of the cost of capital was worked out in the 1950s and 1960s. Each method answers the same old question — what is future cash worth now — with the instruments of its era.

Investment Decisions: NPV & IRR

📖 20 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Every company faces the same question a hundred times a year: should we spend money on this project or not? Build the factory, launch the product, buy the rival? Finance has one right answer to this question — the net present value rule — and a handful of popular shortcuts that mostly agree with it but sometimes fail. This chapter makes you fluent in all of them, and clear about which to trust.

A manager pitches two projects. Project A returns your money in two years; Project B takes four. “A pays back faster,” she says, “so A wins.” But B, over its life, generates twice the value. Choosing A because it “pays back faster” would leave real money on the table. The tools in this chapter exist precisely to stop that mistake — to compare projects by the value they create, not by the story that sounds best in a meeting.

The NPV rule

You already met net present value in Chapter 2. Here it graduates to the central rule of corporate finance. A project's NPV is the present value of all the cash it will generate, minus what it costs today:

The decision rule
NPV = −C₀ + Σ Ct / (1+r)t    // Accept if NPV > 0, reject if NPV < 0

The logic is airtight. A positive NPV means the project is worth more than it costs — taking it makes the owners richer by exactly that amount, in today's dollars. A negative NPV destroys value. When choosing among competing projects, take the one with the highest NPV. No other rule is more fundamental, because NPV directly measures the thing a firm is supposed to maximize: the wealth of its owners. IRR: the project's own rate of return

Managers love a single percentage they can compare to a hurdle. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which a project's NPV equals exactly zero — in effect, the return the project earns on the money invested in it. The IRR rule: accept a project if its IRR exceeds your required return (the “hurdle rate”). A project with a 15% IRR clears a 10% hurdle, so take it.

For a normal project — money out now, money in later — the IRR rule and the NPV rule agree, which is why IRR is so widely used. But IRR has traps. It can give multiple answers when cash flows change sign more than once, and it can mislead when comparing projects of different sizes (a 50% return on $100 beats a 20% return on $1,000 by IRR, but the second creates far more value). When IRR and NPV disagree, trust NPV — it measures value directly, in dollars.

Payback: popular, and flawed

The payback period is how long a project takes to return its initial cost. It is intuitive and widely used, but it has two serious flaws: it ignores the time value of money (a dollar in year 3 counts the same as year 1), and it ignores everything after payback — a project could pay back in two years and then generate a fortune, or nothing, and payback cannot tell the difference. Payback is a fine rough screen for liquidity (“how long is our money tied up?”) but a poor tool for deciding which project creates the most value. That is the manager's mistake from the opening story.

The hierarchy of rules

NPV is the gold standard — it measures value in dollars and never misleads. IRR is a useful companion that usually agrees with NPV but can trip on sign changes and scale. Payback is a quick liquidity check, not a value rule. When they conflict, NPV wins.

Risk & Return

📖 22 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
🎮 1 arena
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Up to now we have discounted cash flows as if we knew them. We don't. The future is uncertain, and finance's central insight about uncertainty is surprising: some risk can be made to vanish for free, simply by not putting all your eggs in one basket. This chapter makes risk and return precise, reveals why diversification is the closest thing to a free lunch in all of finance, and sets up the portfolio theory you'll build by hand in the arena.

Two shops in one town: an umbrella seller and an ice-cream stand. Each has a wildly uncertain year — it depends entirely on the weather. Owned alone, either is a nerve-wracking bet. But own both, and something remarkable happens: rainy months lift umbrellas while sunny months lift ice cream, so your combined income is steady even though each business swings wildly. You did not reduce either business's risk. You cancelled it, by combining risks that move in opposite directions. That is diversification, and it is the single most valuable idea in investing.

Measuring return and risk

To reason about uncertainty we need numbers. The expected return of an investment is the probability-weighted average of its possible returns — your best single guess. The risk is how much the actual return might deviate from that average, measured by the variance or its square root, the standard deviation (often called volatility). A high standard deviation means a wide range of outcomes; a low one means outcomes cluster near the average.

Definition
Standard deviation (volatility)

A measure of how spread out an investment's returns are around their average. A stock with a 20% standard deviation typically swings within about ±20% of its expected return in a given year. It is finance's standard yardstick for the risk of a single asset.

Diversification: the free lunch

Here is the magic. When you combine assets in a portfolio, the portfolio's expected return is simply the weighted average of the individual returns — no surprise there. But the portfolio's risk is generally less than the weighted average of the individual risks, and can be dramatically less if the assets don't move together. The key quantity is correlation: how closely two assets' returns track each other, from +1 (move identically) to −1 (move exactly opposite).

When correlation is below +1, some of the ups and downs cancel out, and the combined volatility falls. The umbrella and ice-cream shops have strongly negative correlation, so together they are nearly riskless. Real stocks are positively but imperfectly correlated, so diversification still cuts risk substantially — without sacrificing expected return. Two kinds of risk — and only one is rewarded

Diversification reveals a profound split. A stock's total risk has two parts. Unsystematic (specific) risk is unique to the company — a factory fire, a lawsuit, a failed product. This risk can be diversified away, because across hundreds of stocks the good and bad surprises cancel out. Systematic (market) risk is shared by all companies — recessions, interest-rate shifts, wars — and cannot be diversified away, because it moves everything at once.

The risk that pays

Because unsystematic risk can be eliminated for free by diversifying, the market does not reward you for bearing it — anyone can shed it. The only risk that earns a premium is systematic risk, the part you cannot escape. This single insight is the foundation of the CAPM (Chapter 9): an asset's expected return depends only on its systematic risk, measured by beta. Hold a diversified portfolio, and you are paid for exactly the risk you cannot avoid.

In the two-asset sheet, set both assets to the same volatility and expected return, then move the correlation from +1 to −1. Describe what happens to portfolio risk and explain why.

Solution

At correlation +1, portfolio risk equals the assets' risk — no benefit, because they move in lockstep. As correlation falls, portfolio risk drops; at −1 with equal weights, risk can fall to nearly zero, because one asset's gains exactly offset the other's losses.

This is diversification in its purest form: same expected return, but risk melts away as the assets become less correlated. Real assets rarely reach −1, but even modest positive correlations leave plenty of risk to diversify.

Same expected return, far less total risk — which is why index funds exist and why Chapter 9's CAPM prices assets by systematic risk alone.

Cost of Capital & Capital Structure

📖 20 min read
🧮 2 live spreadsheets
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Every discount rate we've used so far has been handed to us. This chapter is where it comes from. A company's money is not free — shareholders and lenders both demand a return — and the blended cost of that money, the WACC, is the hurdle rate every project must clear. Along the way we answer finance's most argued-about question: does it matter whether a firm funds itself with debt or equity?

Two identical companies, same factories, same profits. One is funded entirely by shareholders; the other borrows half its money from banks. Are they worth the same? In 1958, two economists proved that in a perfect world — no taxes, no bankruptcy — they are worth exactly the same, because how you slice the pie doesn't change its size. It won them Nobel prizes and it sounds like it settles the debt-versus-equity question. It doesn't — because the real world has taxes and bankruptcy, and those cracks are exactly where the interesting answers live.

The cost of equity: the CAPM

What return do shareholders require? They could invest elsewhere, so they demand at least the risk-free rate plus a premium for the systematic risk they bear (Chapter 8). The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) makes this precise. It says an asset's required return depends on one thing — its beta, a measure of how much the asset moves with the overall market:

The CAPM
Required return = rf + β × (market risk premium)   // rf = risk-free rate; β = systematic risk

The cost of debt — and the tax shield

Debt is cheaper than equity for two reasons. First, lenders bear less risk than shareholders (they get paid first, and have contractual claims), so they demand a lower return — the interest rate on the firm's bonds. Second, and crucially, interest payments are tax-deductible. A company paying 6% interest in a 25% tax bracket has an after-tax cost of debt of only $6\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 4.5\%$. The government effectively subsidizes borrowing. This “tax shield” is the single biggest reason companies use debt at all.

Capital structure: does the debt/equity mix matter?

Since debt is cheaper than equity, why not fund everything with debt and drive the WACC to the floor? This is the capital structure question. The Modigliani–Miller theorem (the two economists from the opening) says that in a perfect world, the mix is irrelevant — adding cheap debt raises the risk to the remaining shareholders, pushing up the cost of equity by exactly enough to cancel the benefit. The pie's size doesn't depend on how you slice it.

The real world breaks the perfection in two directions. The tax shield makes debt genuinely valuable, pulling firms toward more borrowing. But too much debt brings financial distress — the rising risk of bankruptcy, which is costly and frightens customers, suppliers, and employees. The optimal capital structure balances these: enough debt to capture the tax benefit, not so much that distress costs outweigh it. This “trade-off theory” is why real firms carry some debt but rarely fund themselves entirely with it.

Portfolios & Diversification

📖 22 min read
🎮 1 arena
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Chapter 8 showed that diversification cuts risk for free. This chapter follows that idea to its stunning conclusion: out of all possible portfolios, there is a single best one for every investor to hold, and it turns out to be the whole market. That result — worth a Nobel Prize — is why index funds exist and why “buy the market and hold” is the most quietly radical advice in finance.

In 1952, a graduate student named Harry Markowitz asked a question no one had formalized: given a menu of risky assets, which combination is best? His answer — that you should care about how assets move together, not just individually — created modern portfolio theory in a single paper. A decade later, others showed that his logic implies everyone should hold the same risky portfolio: the market itself. That insight, resisted for years by an industry that made its living picking stocks, eventually gave ordinary people the index fund.

The efficient frontier

Take every possible mix of the available risky assets and plot each one by its risk and its expected return. The cloud of possibilities has an upper-left edge — the efficient frontier — consisting of the portfolios that offer the highest return for each level of risk (or equivalently, the lowest risk for each level of return). Any portfolio below the frontier is foolish: you could get more return for the same risk by moving up to the edge. A rational investor holds only frontier portfolios. The Portfolio Builder arena draws this frontier live and lets you find your spot on it.

Why the frontier curves

The frontier bulges toward the upper left precisely because of diversification. If risk were just a weighted average, combinations would fall on straight lines between assets. But because imperfectly correlated assets partly cancel each other's volatility (Chapter 8), the combinations curve leftward — less risk than a simple average would predict. That curve is diversification made visible; the more assets and the lower their correlations, the more it bulges.

Adding a risk-free asset: one best portfolio

Now introduce a risk-free asset — a Treasury bill you can lend at (buy) or borrow at. Something beautiful happens. The best strategy becomes: hold a single specific portfolio of risky assets — the tangency portfolio, the one with the highest Sharpe ratio (return per unit of risk) — and then dial your overall risk up or down by mixing that portfolio with the risk-free asset. Want less risk? Hold more T-bills. Want more? Borrow and buy more of the same portfolio. Everyone holds the same risky portfolio; they differ only in how much cash they blend with it.

Definition
Sharpe ratio

A portfolio's excess return (above the risk-free rate) divided by its volatility — return per unit of risk. The best risky portfolio is the one with the highest Sharpe ratio; chasing it is your goal in the Portfolio Builder arena.

The market portfolio and index funds

If every investor holds the same tangency portfolio, then in equilibrium that portfolio must be the market portfolio — every asset held in proportion to its value. This is the theoretical foundation of index funds: rather than trying to pick winners, simply own the whole market in proportion, cheaply. It also closes the loop with Chapter 9: an individual asset's contribution to the risk of this market portfolio is its beta, which is exactly why beta — not total volatility — determines an asset's expected return in the CAPM.

Sustained, risk-adjusted outperformance over many years is the only evidence of skill — and it is rare. Raw one-year returns, detached from risk and sample size, are close to meaningless.

Market Efficiency & Behavior

📖 20 min read
🎮 1 arena
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Can you beat the market? It is the question every investor secretly asks, and finance has a humbling, well-tested answer: almost never, and not reliably. This chapter explains why prices are so hard to outguess — the efficient-markets hypothesis — examines the strong evidence for it, and then explores the behavioral cracks where human psychology bends prices in ways a patient, disciplined investor can understand.

In 2007, Warren Buffett bet a million dollars that a simple S&P 500 index fund would beat a hand-picked basket of elite hedge funds over ten years. The hedge funds employed armies of analysts, supercomputers, and the sharpest minds on Wall Street, charging enormous fees for their expertise. Ten years later Buffett won, easily. The index fund — owned by anyone, costing almost nothing — beat the experts. That result is not a fluke. It is what the theory in this chapter predicts.

The efficient markets hypothesis

The efficient markets hypothesis (EMH) says that asset prices already reflect all available information. The logic is competitive: if a stock were obviously underpriced, thousands of profit-seeking investors would rush to buy it, pushing the price up until the bargain vanished. So by the time you hear a piece of news, prices have already moved — the information is “in the price.” Prices then change only in response to new, unpredictable information, which is why they look like a random walk: the next move is essentially a coin flip.

Economists distinguish three forms. Weak-form efficiency says past prices can't predict future ones (so chart-reading is futile). Semi-strong says all public information is already priced (so analyzing public financials gives no edge). Strong-form says even private information is priced (which is mostly false — and trading on true inside information is illegal). The practical implication is captured by the semi-strong form: by the time information is public, it is already priced, so publicly known “tips” are worthless.

The evidence: it's hard to beat the market

The EMH makes a sharp, testable prediction: professional money managers, on average, should not beat a simple market index after fees. Decades of data confirm it emphatically. Year after year, the large majority of actively managed funds underperform their benchmark index, and the few that win in one period rarely repeat. The reason is arithmetic as much as efficiency: active investors as a group are the market, so before costs they earn the market return — and after their fees and trading costs, they must on average trail it. This is the “arithmetic of active management,” and it is why low-cost index funds have quietly won.

The behavioral cracks

Markets are efficient, but not perfectly, because they are made of people, and people are predictably irrational. Behavioral finance catalogs the biases that bend prices. Overconfidence makes traders trade too much and lose to fees. Herding drives bubbles as people buy simply because others are buying. Loss aversion makes investors hold losers too long (to avoid crystallizing a loss) and sell winners too early. Anchoring fixes attention on an irrelevant reference point, like the price you paid. Recency bias extrapolates the recent past forever, inflating bubbles and deepening panics.

These biases are real and occasionally move whole markets — the dot-com bubble and the 2008 housing mania are hard to explain without them. But there is a catch that keeps the EMH mostly intact: knowing a bias exists does not make it easy to profit. Bubbles can inflate far longer than a rational skeptic can stay solvent betting against them, and by the time a mispricing is obvious, it is often gone. The practical lesson of behavioral finance is defensive: the biases you can most reliably exploit are your own. Discipline — diversify, keep costs low, hold through panics, ignore tips — beats cleverness.

The honest synthesis

Markets are efficient enough that you should not expect to beat them by trading on public information, yet human enough that prices sometimes swing to extremes. Both are true. The mature investor's response is not to hunt for the market's mistakes but to guard against their own: a low-cost, diversified, long-held portfolio quietly captures the market's return while most active traders, paying fees to act on their biases, fall behind.

Exercise 11.2 Medium

Explain the “arithmetic of active management”: why must active investors, as a group, underperform the market index after costs?

Solution

All investors together own the entire market, so collectively they earn exactly the market return before costs — that is a mathematical identity, not a theory. Index investors capture that return at almost no cost. The remaining (active) investors also collectively earn the market return before costs, but they pay far more in fees and trading expenses.

Therefore, after costs, active investors as a group must earn less than the market — and less than index investors — by exactly the extra costs they incur. Individual active managers can still win, but only at the expense of other active managers, and the average active dollar is doomed to trail. Fees, not stock-picking talent, are the decisive variable.

Reason 2 — the biases are yours too. The same psychology that creates mispricings operates on you. Spotting a bubble intellectually doesn't immunize you from herding into it or panicking out at the bottom. And by the time a mispricing is obvious enough to be certain, arbitrageurs have usually closed it. So behavioral finance better explains market history than it provides a reliable edge — its most actionable advice is defensive: manage your own biases with rules and discipline.

Options & Derivatives

📖 22 min read
🎮 1 arena
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

A derivative is a contract whose value derives from something else — a stock, a currency, a barrel of oil. The most important derivatives are options, which give the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price. That asymmetry — a right without an obligation — makes options extraordinarily flexible: they can insure a portfolio, amplify a bet, or manufacture almost any payoff you can draw. This chapter teaches you to read that geometry, and the arena lets you build it.

A farmer worries the price of wheat will crash before harvest; an airline dreads a spike in jet fuel; a saver fears a market crash right before retirement. Each faces a risk they would happily pay to remove — and for each, a derivative exists that does exactly that, transferring the risk to someone willing to bear it for a price. Options began as insurance for the nervous and tools for the hedged. They can also be used to gamble, spectacularly. Understanding the difference — and the payoff diagram that reveals it — is this chapter's goal.

Calls and puts

Two building blocks generate the entire options universe. A call option gives its holder the right to buy an asset at a fixed strike price before the option expires. You buy a call when you think the price will rise: if the stock soars above the strike, you exercise and pocket the difference; if it doesn't, you simply let the option expire, losing only the premium you paid. A put option is the mirror — the right to sell at the strike — and it gains value when the price falls, making it a form of insurance.

Definition
Call & put options

A call is the right (not obligation) to buy at the strike price; it profits when the underlying rises. A put is the right to sell at the strike; it profits when the underlying falls. The buyer pays a premium up front; the maximum loss for a buyer is that premium, while the upside can be large.

Reading the payoff diagram

The clearest way to understand any option position is its payoff diagram: profit or loss (vertical) against the underlying's price at expiration (horizontal). A long call is flat at a small loss (the premium) until the strike, then slopes upward — limited loss, large upside. A long put slopes the opposite way — it pays off as the price falls. Because you can combine calls, puts, and the stock itself, you can assemble almost any shape: a straddle (buy a call and a put) that profits from a big move in either direction; a covered call that trades away upside for income; a protective put that caps your downside like an insurance policy. Hedging vs. speculation — and leverage

The same option can be insurance or a lottery ticket, depending on who holds it and why. A retiree who buys puts on her stock portfolio is hedging — paying a premium to cap her downside, exactly like buying insurance. A trader who buys far-out-of-the-money calls with his savings is speculating — using the option's leverage (a small premium controls a large position) to make an amplified, all-or-nothing bet. Options are neither good nor evil; they are tools that transfer and reshape risk. The leverage that makes them powerful for hedging is the same leverage that makes them dangerous for gamblers.

Put-call parity: options are connected

Calls and puts are not independent — they are bound together by an elegant no-arbitrage relationship called put-call parity. It says that a call and a put with the same strike and expiration are linked to the stock and a risk-free bond, so that if you know three of the four prices, the fourth is pinned down exactly. The deep idea is that you can replicate one instrument by combining the others: owning a stock plus a protective put produces the same payoff as owning a call plus cash. If the prices ever drifted out of line, arbitrageurs would instantly trade the difference away. You don't need the formula for this course, but the principle — that derivatives are tied to their underlying by the impossibility of free money — is the beating heart of all derivative pricing, including the famous Black-Scholes model that the Options Lab uses to price every contract.

Why derivatives can be dangerous at scale

Leverage cuts both ways. Because a small premium controls a large exposure, derivatives let institutions take enormous risks with little visible capital — which is wonderful until it isn't. Much of the 2008 crisis traced to derivatives (mortgage-linked contracts) whose risks were poorly understood and enormous in scale. The tools that let a farmer sleep at night can, misused, threaten the whole system. Respect the leverage.

Budgeting, Credit & Debt

📖 18 min read
🧮 1 live spreadsheet
✎ 3 exercises + quiz

Now we turn every tool in this book onto the one portfolio you will manage your whole life: your own. The theory is the same — cash flows, compounding, discount rates — but the stakes are personal. This chapter is about the foundation everything else rests on: spending less than you earn, understanding how credit really works, and dismantling debt efficiently. Get these right and the wealth-building of Chapter 14 becomes almost automatic.

Two people earn the same $60,000 salary. One finishes each year with $8,000 saved and no debt; the other finishes with a $6,000 credit-card balance growing at 22%. Within a decade their financial lives are unrecognizably different — one compounding wealth, the other compounding a debt that quietly consumes a month's salary a year in interest alone. The difference was never income. It was the gap between money in and money out, and what they did with it. That gap is the whole game, and it is entirely within your control.

Budgeting: the cash-flow statement of your life

A budget is nothing more exotic than a personal cash-flow statement: money in, money out, and the difference. The difference — your savings — is the raw material of every future dollar of wealth, because it is what you feed into the compounding machine of Chapter 3. The single most effective budgeting habit is to invert the usual order: instead of spending and saving what's left, save first and spend what's left — “pay yourself first” — by automating a transfer to savings the day you're paid. A common starting framework is the 50/30/20 rule: roughly 50% of after-tax income to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings and debt repayment. The exact split matters less than the discipline of having one.

The only equation that matters

Wealth is built from the gap between what you earn and what you spend, invested and left to compound. You can widen that gap from either side — earn more or spend less — but the gap itself, not the income, is what compounds. A high earner with no gap builds nothing; a modest earner with a steady gap and forty years builds a fortune (Chapter 3 proved it). Everything else in personal finance is a detail on top of this one equation.

How credit cards really work

A credit card is a short-term loan at a punishing interest rate — often 20% or more APR, compounding monthly. Used as a convenience and paid in full each month, it is free and even profitable (rewards, fraud protection). Carried as a balance, it is one of the most expensive debts available to ordinary people. The trap is the minimum payment: it is deliberately set just above the monthly interest, so paying only the minimum can take decades to clear a balance, with total interest often exceeding the original purchase. Good debt, bad debt, and how to attack it

Not all debt is equal. “Good” debt finances something that builds value or income — a mortgage on a home, a student loan for a degree that raises earnings, a loan for a productive business — usually at a modest rate. “Bad” debt finances consumption at a high rate — credit-card balances, payday loans, financing a depreciating car you can't afford. The distinction is really about the interest rate versus what the borrowed money earns you: borrowing at 22% to buy things that produce nothing is wealth moving in reverse.

When attacking multiple debts, two strategies compete. The avalanche pays minimums on everything and throws every extra dollar at the highest-interest debt first — mathematically optimal, minimizing total interest. The snowball attacks the smallest balance first — slightly more expensive, but the quick early wins build momentum and motivation. The avalanche saves the most money; the snowball helps more people actually finish. The best strategy is the one you will stick with.

Exercise 13.2 Medium

In the payoff calculator, a $6,000 balance at 22% APR. Compare paying $150/month versus $300/month. Roughly how much time and interest does doubling the payment save?

Solution

At $150/month the balance takes roughly 5+ years to clear and costs several thousand dollars in interest; at $300/month it clears in about 2 years with far less interest — the calculator shows the exact figures. Doubling the payment more than halves both the time and the interest, because you spend far less time paying interest on a lingering balance.

The lesson: on high-interest debt, every extra dollar of payment is a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to the card's APR — a 22% “investment” you cannot beat anywhere else. Paying down a 22% card is the best risk-free return in personal finance.

Exercise 13.3 Hard

A friend has $3,000 in a savings account earning 4% and a $3,000 credit-card balance charging 22%. He's proud of his savings. What should he almost certainly do, and why?

Solution

He should use most of the savings to pay off the credit card immediately. He is earning 4% on his savings while paying 22% on his debt — a net loss of about 18% a year on $3,000, roughly $540 annually, for the comfort of seeing a savings balance.

Paying off the 22% card is equivalent to earning a guaranteed, risk-free, tax-free 22% return — far better than any investment. The one caveat: he should keep a small emergency buffer (so he doesn't re-borrow at 22% the next time the car breaks), and he must not simply run the balance back up. But holding low-yield savings alongside high-rate debt is one of the most common and costly mistakes in personal finance.

Building Wealth & Retirement

This is where every thread in the book ties together. Compounding (Chapter 3), diversification (Chapters 8 and 10), market efficiency (Chapter 11), and the budgeting discipline of Chapter 13 combine into a single, almost boring, and astonishingly effective plan for building wealth over a lifetime. There is no secret. The secret is that there is no secret — only a handful of well-understood moves, started early and repeated for decades.

The most reliable millionaires in America are rarely the highest earners — they are teachers, engineers, and small-business owners who did a few ordinary things relentlessly: they spent less than they earned, invested the difference in low-cost diversified funds, captured every tax break and employer match, and then did the hardest thing of all — nothing, for thirty years, through every crash and boom. This chapter is that plan, built from the tools you now understand, and the arena lets you run your own version of it to retirement.

The wealth formula

Strip personal wealth-building to its core and it is four verbs: earn, save, invest, wait. Earn an income; save a portion of it (Chapter 13's gap); invest that savings in a diversified, low-cost portfolio (Chapters 10–11); and let compounding work for decades (Chapter 3). Each step is necessary and none is sufficient alone — a great income with no saving builds nothing, and diligent saving stuffed under a mattress is eaten by inflation. It is the combination, sustained over time, that produces wealth. The single most powerful variable, as Chapter 3 showed, is time: the earlier you start, the more of the work compounding does for you.

Use the tax-advantaged accounts first

The government offers powerful incentives to save for retirement, and using them is the closest thing to free money in finance. A 401(k) (through an employer) and an IRA (on your own) let your investments grow shielded from taxes. In a traditional account you deduct contributions now and pay tax on withdrawals later; in a Roth you pay tax now and withdraw tax-free in retirement. Either way, decades of compounding happen untaxed, which dramatically increases the final balance versus an ordinary taxable account.

And then there is the employer match — genuinely free money. Many employers match your 401(k) contributions up to some percentage of salary. Not contributing enough to get the full match is turning down a guaranteed, instant 100% return on that money. It is the first dollar anyone should invest, before paying down anything but the highest-rate debt. The live sheet shows what the match is worth once it compounds:

Asset allocation over a lifetime

What should you actually own inside these accounts? For most people, the answer is gloriously simple: a broad, low-cost index fund (Chapter 10), or a handful of them spanning stocks and bonds. The one dial worth turning is the stock/bond mix, and it should shift with your time horizon. When retirement is decades away, you can hold mostly stocks — their volatility is irrelevant when you won't touch the money for thirty years, and their higher expected return compounds enormously. As retirement nears, you gradually shift toward bonds and cash, because a crash the year before you retire is a real danger, not a distant abstraction. This gradual shift is called a glide path, and “target-date” funds do it automatically.

The whole plan, in one place

Here is the entire book, turned into a personal plan. First, build a small emergency fund (a few months' expenses) so a surprise doesn't send you back to 22% debt. Second, pay off high-interest debt — a guaranteed return nothing can beat (Chapter 13). Third, contribute enough to get the full employer match — free money. Fourth, invest steadily in low-cost, diversified funds inside tax-advantaged accounts, with a stock/bond mix suited to your horizon. Fifth, and hardest, hold through the storms — do not sell in panics, do not chase fads, do not try to beat a market you now understand is nearly impossible to beat (Chapter 11). Then let time and compounding do the rest.

Start early, keep it simple, and let compounding, the quiet engine of Chapter 3, carry you the rest of the way.

Exercise 14.1 Easy

You earn $50,000 and your employer matches 100% up to 4% of salary. If you contribute 4%, what is your total annual retirement contribution?

Solution

You contribute 4% × 50,000 = $2,000; the employer adds another $2,000. Total: $4,000 — the employer instantly doubled your money before a cent of investment return. Contributing less than 4% would leave part of that free $2,000 on the table.

Exercise 14.2 Medium

Why is the order “emergency fund → high-interest debt → capture the match → invest broadly” sensible? What could go wrong if you skip the emergency fund?

Solution

The order follows guaranteed returns and risk. High-interest debt payoff is a guaranteed ~20% return (Chapter 13); the employer match is a guaranteed ~100% return — both beat any market investment, so they come first. Broad investing comes after, for the long-run market return.

Skipping the emergency fund is dangerous because the first surprise expense — a car repair, a medical bill, a job loss — would force you to borrow at high rates or sell investments at a bad time, undoing your progress. The buffer keeps a temporary shock from becoming permanent 22% debt. It earns little, but it protects everything else.

Exercise 14.3 Hard

A colleague pulled all her retirement money into cash after a 30% market crash, “to be safe,” and plans to reinvest “once things calm down.” Using several ideas from this book, explain why this is likely a costly mistake.

Solution

Selling low locks in the loss (Chapter 8, 13): a paper loss becomes real only when you sell. By moving to cash after the fall, she converts a temporary decline into a permanent loss and gives up the recovery.

Market timing rarely works (Chapter 11): “once things calm down” means after prices have already risen — she'll likely buy back higher than she sold. The best days in the market cluster near the worst ones, and missing a handful of them devastates long-run returns. Efficient markets make the reinvestment point unknowable.

Her horizon makes volatility survivable (Chapter 3, 10): if retirement is years away, a 30% crash is noise that compounding will overwhelm — provided she stays invested. The disciplined move is to hold (and keep contributing, buying at lower prices). The single behavior that most separates successful investors from unsuccessful ones is not selling in panics — the defensive lesson of behavioral finance applied to the highest-stakes moment.
